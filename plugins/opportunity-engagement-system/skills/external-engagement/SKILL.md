# External Engagement

## Core purpose

Use External Engagement as the field-learning and relationship-development workflow around opportunity selection and before resource commitment. It has two operating modes: Opportunity Formation and Partnership Conversion.

## Field boundary

Own field learning and relationship conversion, not portfolio selection or material resource allocation.

The governing question is:

**What must be learned, secured, or converted in the external field before the opportunity can be strategically re-screened or materially resourced?**

Treat the principal uncertainty here as **field/relationship uncertainty**: the unknown most likely to change the diagnosis of the problem, decision owner, sponsor, demand, access, reciprocity, role, ownership, attribution, governance, or partnership readiness.

Do not re-score strategic attractiveness merely because a counterpart is enthusiastic, senior, prestigious, accessible, or willing to collaborate.

Return material strategic evidence to Strategic Opportunity for re-screening.

Treat the following as relationship positions rather than a mandatory linear funnel:

`external access -> trust -> diagnosed problem -> decision owner -> sponsor -> co-definition -> ownership -> reciprocal commitment -> funding or impact mechanism -> repeat relationship`

An engagement can begin at any credible position, move backward when evidence changes, combine positions in one interaction, or stop without conversion.

Optimise for durable external assets rather than activity count. The desired result is not merely a meeting, logo, conference appearance, student project, or inclusion in another academic's relationship. The desired result is a direct and attributable relationship that can produce research, consulting, funding, policy influence, data, reputation, referrals, or other cumulative assets.

Default to the user's language. When responding in Chinese, retain useful terms such as ownership, problem owner, sponsor, economic buyer, broker, gateway, resource readiness, conversion, reciprocal commitment, free-work boundary, policy window, honest broker, attribution, handoff, and pipeline.

## Boundary with other skills

Keep the three systems distinct.

### Strategic Opportunity

Answer:

- Is the opportunity strategically attractive?
- Does it deserve portfolio entry?
- Does it strengthen a cumulative arena?
- Is the user's role, control, and ownership potential sufficient?

Use Strategic Opportunity for a preliminary screen before costly engagement and for a re-screen after material field evidence. When the principal uncertainty cannot be resolved from current evidence, transfer it to Opportunity Formation rather than treating the preliminary verdict as final. Do not recreate portfolio scoring here.

### External Engagement

Answer:

- Does the immediate uncertainty require Opportunity Formation or Partnership Conversion?
- Who should the user engage?
- Through which access route?
- What stage is the relationship at?
- What should the next conversation accomplish?
- Who owns the problem and who can mobilise resources or authority?
- How can the user convert borrowed access into a direct, attributable relationship?
- What reciprocal commitment, funding route, policy pathway, or output should follow?

### Adaptive Commitment

Answer:

- How much time, attention, money, and persistence should be committed now?
- What is the resource cap, milestone, review condition, and scale/park/kill rule?

Use the de minimis engagement rule for one bounded, low-cost evidence-producing interaction. Once the next action exceeds that boundary and requires material time, protected attention, money, staffing, delivery effort, or persistence, hand off to Adaptive Commitment for resource architecture. Do not set a full execution budget or persistence period here.

### Evidence Verification

Use Evidence Verification for decision-critical documentary facts such as current roles, formal program conditions, public commitments, filings, dates, organisational status, or counterpart statements when those facts are disputed, stale, source-conflicted, or materially consequential.

Do not outsource field learning to Evidence Verification. A document cannot establish actual sponsor willingness, reciprocity, trust, access, role acceptance, or future commitment when those require observable counterpart behaviour or conversation. Return those questions to Opportunity Formation or Partnership Conversion.

Routine factual checks may remain inside External Engagement. Use the separate verification capability only when claim-level error could materially change the engagement route, counterpart map, ownership position, or next move.

## Operating modes

Choose the mode from the immediate uncertainty. Do not assume a fixed lifecycle.

### Opportunity Formation

Use when a named actor, contact, policy process, or field exists but the consequential problem, owner, response heterogeneity, evidence gap, access pathway, or research opportunity remains unclear.

Produce:

- external change or strategic-response episode;
- consequential practice decision;
- observed or suspected response heterogeneity;
- problem-owner and sponsor hypotheses;
- evidence gap and access hypothesis;
- candidate practice problem and research puzzle;
- evidence required for Strategic Opportunity re-screen;
- formation stop rule.

The formation stop rule must prevent progression when there is no consequential problem, credible owner, plausible access pathway, or combined research and practice value.

### Partnership Conversion

Use when a candidate has passed a preliminary strategic screen and the immediate task is to form a reciprocal, attributable, governed collaboration.

Produce:

- jointly recognised practice question and research puzzle;
- counterpart and user roles;
- problem, decision, data, and implementation access;
- reciprocal commitment;
- relationship, problem, project, and output ownership;
- governance, independence, and attribution position;
- funding or impact mechanism;
- Adaptive Commitment handoff;
- conversion stop rule.

The conversion stop rule must trigger redesign, monitoring, or exit when the user's role is peripheral, reciprocity or substantive access is absent, ownership and attribution cannot be protected, or governance would compromise independence.

## Mode selection and switching

One interaction may generate Formation and Conversion evidence. Start with the uncertainty that governs the interaction. Switch modes only when observable evidence changes the state, and record that evidence in the transition log. Return to Formation when a proposed partnership exposes an unresolved problem, owner, access, or evidence gap.

Broad environmental scanning is Agent-and-tool work, followed by a Strategic Opportunity preliminary screen; it is not a third External Engagement mode. Policy/Public Influence remains a substantive track and can operate within Formation or Conversion.

## Scope

Use this skill for:

- direct approaches to companies, government bodies, regulators, industry associations, professional bodies, NGOs, and community organisations;
- warm introductions through colleagues, centres, alumni, advisory boards, mentors, or existing partners;
- intermediaries and brokers such as industry growth centres, adoption centres, chambers of commerce, research translation bodies, and government innovation agencies;
- public consultation, regulatory submission, policy roundtable, expert briefing, advisory, and public-interest engagement;
- reactivation of dormant external relationships;
- conversion of teaching or student-project clients into research-capable partners;
- mapping external networks and identifying high-value pathways;
- deciding whether an organisation is a payer/resource owner, gateway/influence node, both, or neither;
- preparing an outreach strategy, conversation agenda, follow-up note, conversion test, or relationship ledger entry.

Do not use this skill merely to maximise the number of contacts. Do not treat generic networking, logo accumulation, unpaid delivery, or grant-deadline partner hunting as successful engagement.

## Operating principles

1. **Borrow the introduction; build the direct relationship.** Treat a colleague, centre, broker, or institution as an access route, not the permanent owner of every subsequent interaction.
2. **Ownership is relational and attributable, not exclusive.** Preserve the introducer's credit and institutional obligations while ensuring that the external actor knows the user's expertise, communicates directly with the user, and can identify the user's contribution.
3. **Distinguish access from ownership.** A board seat, centre logo, old student project, or copied email does not establish the user's relationship ownership.
4. **Distinguish the problem owner from the sponsor.** Identify who experiences and understands the problem, then identify who can authorise cash, staff time, data, access, in-kind support, procurement, or policy action.
5. **Prefer persistent, consequential problems.** Downgrade one-semester tasks, generic dashboards, and opportunistic data requests unless they lead to a repeated decision, research question, or organisational capability.
6. **Trust must become reciprocal and progressively resource-bearing.** As the relationship develops, the counterpart should contribute executive time, data, staff, introductions, in-kind support, cash, access, formal endorsement, or policy attention.
7. **Free scoping is not free delivery.** Offer bounded conversation, diagnosis, and framing. Do not provide a usable report, model, analysis, survey, policy position, application, or repeated workshop without an explicit mechanism and reciprocal commitment.
8. **Grant is a funding vehicle, not the starting relationship.** Avoid asking a near-stranger to join a short-deadline proposal before problem and sponsor alignment exists.
9. **Resource readiness matters more than sector wealth alone.** A wealthy sector without a sponsor can be a poor target. A smaller organisation with a senior champion, persistent problem, data, co-investment, and gateway value can be strong.
10. **Separate teaching engagement from research conversion.** A pro-bono student project may be excellent teaching but should not be described as funded research or a personally owned industry relationship.
11. **Policy engagement is not judged only by payment.** Evaluate decision access, policy relevance, independence, public credibility, stakeholder reach, and attributable influence.
12. **Protect independence and legitimacy.** Do not let engagement turn the user into an undisclosed lobbyist, captured advocate, ghost consultant, or provider of uncredited labour.
13. **Build a promotion-ready evidence trail.** Record initiation, leadership, problem definition, resources secured, outputs, external use, follow-on activity, and attributable impact.

## Intake and verification

Proceed when the available information is sufficient. Otherwise state assumptions and mark the assessment provisional.

Useful inputs include:

1. organisation, person, or policy process;
2. why the engagement matters now;
3. access route: direct, warm institutional, intermediary, public visibility, or prior relationship;
4. current relationship stage and history;
5. suspected business or policy problem;
6. known problem owner, decision authority, sponsor, buyer, or gatekeeper;
7. signals of cash, in-kind support, data, staff time, access, or policy attention;
8. the user's intended role and desired ownership position;
9. expected outputs: conversation, research, consultancy, grant, data, publication, submission, advisory role, referral, or public impact;
10. confidentiality, conflict, IP, editorial, procurement, lobbying, or university-governance constraints.

Before recommending a named contact or current organisation path, verify current roles and organisational status when they may have changed. Use uploaded documents, connected communications, and public sources as appropriate. Clearly separate documented evidence from inference.
When such a factual check is decision-critical, disputed, or source-conflicted, use Evidence Verification. When the missing evidence requires counterpart behaviour, conversation, willingness, or access, keep the question inside External Engagement.

## Route the engagement

Choose one operating mode, one substantive track, and one access mode. The operating mode answers the lifecycle uncertainty; the track and access mode answer the engagement context.

### Substantive tracks

- **Industry/commercial:** company, investor, buyer, technology provider, infrastructure operator, professional-services firm, or commercially oriented association. Load `references/industry-track.md`.
- **Policy/public:** government, regulator, public consultation, public agency, policy network, community body, advocacy setting, or public-interest engagement. Load `references/policy-public-track.md`.
- **Hybrid:** a business-policy problem involving regulation, industry transition, public funding, nonmarket strategy, or government–industry coordination. Load both track references and keep commercial and public-interest objectives distinct.

### Access modes

- **Direct:** the user approaches the external decision-maker without an intermediary.
- **Warm institutional:** a colleague, mentor, research centre, board, alumni network, or existing university relationship opens the door.
- **Broker/intermediary:** an organisation searches, matches, translates, convenes, or introduces across a wider network.
- **Public credibility:** a publication, consultation submission, public talk, expert commentary, or policy contribution creates legitimacy for contact.
- **Reactivation:** a prior relationship exists but the contact, sponsor, or institutional connection has weakened.

## Relationship-development positions

Use the positions below to diagnose a relationship and design the next evidence-producing move. They can overlap, repeat, regress, or stop. Do not skip directly from access to a proposal unless the relationship and problem are already mature.

### 1. Access

Identify the legitimate route to a named person or decision process.

Ask:

- Why should this person take the meeting?
- What credibility transfers through the introducer, broker, prior work, or public contribution?
- Is the route likely to produce a direct conversation, or only mediated contact?
- Is the user approaching a relevant senior person rather than a generic inbox?

Output the best access path and the reason it is credible.

### 2. Trust

Design a low-cost, reciprocal first interaction.

Aim to demonstrate:

- domain understanding;
- intellectual independence;
- accurate listening;
- discretion and reliability;
- a useful way of framing the issue without prematurely prescribing a solution.

Do not treat trust-building as permission for substantial unpaid work.

### 3. Diagnose

Determine whether there is a real and persistent problem.

Ask:

- What decision, process, constraint, risk, or policy friction recurs?
- What is the cost of delay, error, non-compliance, weak adoption, or poor coordination?
- Is the issue important to the organisation or mainly interesting to the academic?
- What has already been tried?
- Why has the problem persisted?
- Which evidence would change a real decision?

Translate surface requests into underlying mechanisms where justified.

### 4. Identify the decision owner

Name the person or unit accountable for the decision or problem.

Do not assume that the first enthusiastic contact owns the problem. Distinguish:

- user of the eventual work;
- operational or policy problem owner;
- technical expert;
- gatekeeper;
- procurement or governance function;
- executive or political decision authority.

### 5. Identify the sponsor

Name the person who can mobilise resources or authority.

For industry, test cash, in-kind support, staff time, data access, procurement, grant co-investment, and executive sponsorship.

For policy/public work, test agenda access, commissioning authority, formal consultation status, briefing access, convening power, public endorsement, implementation authority, and follow-up channels.

A strong problem without a sponsor is not yet a project.

### 6. Co-define

Convert the practical or policy issue into a jointly understood problem.

Produce a concise problem architecture:

- current decision or process;
- persistent friction;
- proposed mechanism;
- evidence required;
- stakeholders and affected groups;
- feasible scope;
- potential outputs;
- unresolved assumptions.

Avoid converting the relationship into an academic-led proposal before the counterpart recognises the problem definition.

### 7. Formalise ownership and attribution

Clarify the user's role before substantial work begins.


External Engagement assesses **actual field ownership and attribution**, based on observable relationship evidence.

Distinguish:

- direct relationship ownership;
- recognised problem-definition role;
- project leadership or clearly bounded specialist responsibility;
- output attribution;
- legitimate access rights;
- reciprocal counterpart commitment.

Do not infer ownership from strategic importance, prestige, anticipated future contribution, or the user's willingness to invest effort.

Pass ownership evidence upstream or downstream as evidence. Do not convert it into a portfolio-selection or resource-allocation decision inside this Skill.

Assess four forms of ownership:

1. **Relationship ownership:** the external actor communicates directly with the user and would contact the user again.
2. **Problem ownership:** the user has a recognised role in defining and advancing the question.
3. **Project ownership:** the user is a lead, co-lead, or clearly bounded specialist rather than invisible support.
4. **Output attribution:** contracts, grants, reports, publications, submissions, acknowledgements, impact records, and public materials identify the user's contribution accurately.

Ownership need not be exclusive. It must be sufficiently direct, central, and visible to create a durable career and network asset.

### 8. Require reciprocal commitment

Before delivery, identify what the counterpart will contribute.

Acceptable signals include:

- named staff and executive time;
- proprietary or operational data with governance support;
- cash or procurement pathway;
- formal in-kind commitment;
- a co-funded grant pathway;
- access to sites, users, customers, suppliers, or stakeholders;
- a named introduction to the resource holder;
- formal policy briefing, consultation access, or advisory participation;
- permission and support for attributable publication or impact evidence.

Repeated enthusiasm without resource contribution is weak evidence.

### 9. Select the funding or impact mechanism

Match the mechanism to the problem and relationship maturity.

Possible mechanisms include:

- consultancy or advisory contract;
- contract research or sponsored research;
- ARC Linkage, RDC, CRC, mission-oriented, government, philanthropic, or industry co-funded grant;
- funded PhD, postdoctoral position, internship, or executive education;
- data partnership or case-study research;
- policy submission, commissioned briefing, expert workshop, advisory panel, roundtable, or public report;
- research publication, decision tool, diagnostic, dataset, framework, or implementation evaluation.

Do not force every relationship into a grant. Do not call a policy outcome unsuccessful merely because no payment occurs.

### 10. Repeat and compound

After a credible output or contribution:

- establish the next direct interaction;
- ask for one relevant referral where appropriate;
- convert the case into reusable theory, data, method, evidence, reputation, or teaching material;
- document impact and attribution;
- reduce dependence on the original introducer;
- decide whether to deepen, broaden, maintain, or exit the relationship.

## Five-question conversion dashboard

For each node, answer these questions using **Yes / Partial / No / Unknown** and cite the evidence.

1. **Senior decision-maker relationship:** Is there a direct or realistically accessible relationship with a senior problem owner, decision authority, or sponsor?
2. **Pay or co-invest signal:** Has the organisation shown willingness to contribute cash, procurement, in-kind resources, staff, data, access, formal endorsement, or implementation authority?
3. **Persistent problem:** Is there a repeated, consequential business or policy problem rather than a one-semester analytical task?
4. **Prior conversion:** Has the relationship produced a grant, consultancy, contract research, publication, proprietary-data research, commissioned work, policy use, implementation, or another substantive output?
5. **Gateway value:** Can the person or organisation introduce the user to someone who owns resources, authority, or a stronger network?

Do not reward relationship duration, semester count, conference attendance, or logo visibility unless they support one of these five questions.

Classify the node:

- **Resource-ready:** credible problem, owner, sponsor, and reciprocal resource pathway.
- **Gateway:** limited direct funding but strong introduction, convening, or policy influence value.
- **Both:** resource-ready and a high-value gateway.
- **Conversion test:** promising signals, but one explicit test is needed before further investment.
- **Teaching/service-only:** useful for teaching, duty, or goodwill but not a research or funded-engagement pipeline.
- **Monitor/archive:** weak evidence on all five questions; maintain only light touch unless conditions change.



## De minimis engagement rule

External Engagement may recommend and design one bounded, low-cost, evidence-producing interaction without an Adaptive Commitment handoff when all of the following are true:

- the action is reversible;
- it does not require protected deep-work time or material expenditure;
- it remains within the free-work boundary;
- it is primarily designed to resolve a field or relationship uncertainty;
- it does not create an open-ended obligation;
- failure would create little material opportunity cost.

Typical de minimis actions include:

- one concise outreach message;
- one exploratory conversation of roughly 30-60 minutes;
- reasonable preparation using existing or public material;
- one short follow-up;
- a one-page problem framing or question set.

Hand off to Adaptive Commitment when the next action requires material preparation, repeated engagement, substantive bespoke analysis or delivery, application or proposal work, a pilot or prototype, protected research time, financial expenditure, staff or student allocation, multi-week persistence, or another meaningful resource commitment.

Do not invoke Adaptive Commitment merely because every interaction technically consumes some time or attention.

## Free-work boundary

Permit bounded trust-building activities:

- one substantive exploratory conversation;
- reasonable preparation based on public information;
- a short follow-up summary;
- a one-page problem framing or question set;
- a tightly scoped diagnostic discussion or workshop when reciprocal attendance and access are clear.

Treat the following as delivery requiring an explicit mechanism and Adaptive Commitment review:

- data cleaning, analysis, modelling, coding, survey design, interview work, or evaluation;
- a report, business case, policy position, submission written for another organisation, or implementation plan;
- repeated workshops or meetings without progression;
- grant, tender, procurement, or consultancy drafting for an organisation that has not committed resources;
- substantial student labour presented as research conversion;
- bespoke recommendations that the counterpart can implement without further engagement.

When the boundary is crossed, state it directly and propose the appropriate funded, co-invested, formal public-interest, or service-track mechanism.

## Hard gates and exit conditions

Downgrade, redesign, or stop when any of the following applies:

- no persistent problem or decision consequence;
- no path to a named problem owner or sponsor;
- the contact remains enthusiastic but cannot mobilise resources or authority;
- the relationship remains permanently mediated, with no path to direct substantive contact or visible attribution;
- the user is included only as peripheral labour in another person's relationship;
- repeated requests for free delivery;
- the organisation returns only for pro-bono student work and shows no conversion signal;
- a short grant deadline is the main reason for seeking a partner;
- no reciprocal commitment after a reasonable conversion test;
- the work creates material conflict, lobbying, confidentiality, authorship, IP, procurement, editorial, or reputational risk;
- policy engagement requires undisclosed advocacy for a private interest;
- public claims of influence or partnership cannot be evidenced;
- the opportunity cannot produce relationship, research, policy, reputational, data, capability, or economic assets proportionate to the user's role.

Do not infer bad faith merely because an organisation declines to pay. Classify the relationship accurately and allocate attention accordingly.

## Outreach and conversation design

When the user requests an approach strategy, specify:

1. the named person or role;
2. why that person is relevant;
3. the access route;
4. the purpose of the first conversation;
5. two to five diagnostic questions;
6. what not to offer yet;
7. the conversion signal to seek;
8. the next-step test.

When the user explicitly requests an email or message, draft it only after the logic is clear. Keep first contact short, specific, and conversation-seeking. Avoid generic sales language, inflated claims, attachment-heavy pitches, and premature funding requests. For a warm introduction, acknowledge the introducer without implying that the relationship or opportunity already belongs to the user.

## Default outputs

### Opportunity Formation output

Provide:

- current opportunity state, Formation mode, and confidence;
- documented evidence, inference, and missing evidence;
- external change or strategic-response episode;
- consequential practice decision and suspected response heterogeneity;
- problem-owner and sponsor hypotheses;
- access hypothesis and evidence gap;
- candidate practice problem and research puzzle;
- evidence required for Strategic Opportunity re-screen;
- next evidence-producing interaction;
- formation stop rule;
- concise relationship-ledger update.

### Partnership Conversion output

Provide:

- current opportunity state, Conversion mode, and confidence;
- jointly recognised practice question and research puzzle;
- role, access, reciprocity, ownership, attribution, governance, and independence position;
- funding or impact mechanism;
- next conversion test;
- conversion stop rule;
- Adaptive Commitment handoff when a concrete resource decision exists;
- concise relationship-ledger update.

### Full engagement assessment

Provide:

- route and current relationship stage;
- conversion verdict and confidence;
- five-question dashboard;
- documented evidence versus inference;
- problem owner and sponsor map;
- user's current and desired ownership position;
- persistent problem hypothesis;
- recommended next action;
- conversation objective and questions;
- reciprocal commitment to seek;
- free-work boundary;
- plausible funding or impact mechanisms;
- conflict, independence, attribution, and governance risks;
- stop or downgrade condition;
- Strategic Opportunity or Adaptive Commitment handoff, if required;
- concise relationship-ledger entry using `references/relationship-ledger.md`.

### Quick pass

Provide only:

- track and access mode;
- current stage;
- best next action;
- problem owner or sponsor gap;
- ownership risk;
- conversion signal;
- stop rule.

### Network map

For multiple nodes, use the fields in `references/relationship-ledger.md`. Rank pathways, not merely organisations. Distinguish active, conversion-test, gateway, teaching/service-only, dormant, and archive nodes.

### Meeting preparation

Provide:

- governing operating mode and current opportunity state;
- meeting objective;
- counterpart map;
- problem hypotheses;
- diagnostic questions;
- evidence to bring;
- claims to avoid;
- commitment to seek;
- closing question and follow-up action.

### Post-meeting review

Separate:

- prior state and mode;
- what was learned;
- evidence of problem significance;
- evidence of authority and resources;
- direct relationship progress;
- reciprocal commitment;
- ownership and attribution position;
- evidence that justifies staying in the current mode or switching modes;
- updated evidence version and transition-log entry;
- next action;
- Strategic Opportunity re-screen, Adaptive Commitment handoff, or exit condition.

## Handoff rules

### To Strategic Opportunity

Hand off when the relationship reveals a new project, grant, consultancy, dataset, policy initiative, venture, or research direction whose strategic attractiveness is not yet established.

Transfer:

- current Agent state, External Engagement mode, and evidence version;
- opportunity definition;
- deeper arena;
- problem and decision owner;
- sponsor and resource signals;
- user's prospective role and ownership;
- access and evidence quality;
- documented evidence, inference, and missing evidence;
- expected assets;
- main conflict or governance risk.

### To Adaptive Commitment

Hand off when a concrete next step is justified and requires a resource cap, milestone, persistence rule, or protected time.

Transfer:

- current Agent state, External Engagement mode, and evidence version;
- current engagement stage;
- next action;
- principal uncertainty;
- counterpart commitment;
- ownership position;
- free-work boundary;
- evidence that would justify deeper commitment;
- exit or downgrade trigger.

# Claim Status and Confidence Calibration

## Separate three judgements

Always distinguish:

1. **Status:** what the evidence supports about the claim.
2. **Confidence:** how robust that status judgement is.
3. **Decision impact:** how much the result matters to the downstream decision.

Do not collapse them into one score.

## Status definitions

### Verified

Use when:

- evidence is source-fit and sufficiently current;
- the material wording is directly supported;
- key qualifiers are supported rather than inferred;
- no unresolved stronger contradiction remains.

Do not require perfect omniscience.

### Partially verified

Use when:

- the central proposition is supported;
- one or more material qualifiers are too broad, stale, ambiguous, or unsupported;
- a narrower formulation is defensible.

Typical qualifiers that cause partial verification:

- date;
- number;
- exclusivity;
- binding status;
- geography;
- legal status;
- causality;
- generality;
- identity of the counterparty or entity.

### Unsupported

Use when:

- the search did not find adequate evidence for the proposition;
- available material merely repeats the claim without establishing it;
- only weak or unsourced sources support it;
- a stronger record should exist but has not been located, without enough basis to call the claim false.

State explicitly that Unsupported is not equivalent to Contradicted.

### Contradicted

Use when:

- stronger, more current, or more authoritative evidence conflicts with the material proposition;
- an official record rules out the claimed status;
- the claim relies on a superseded version that no longer applies;
- the actual figure, entity, date, or legal status materially differs.

Explain the contradiction rather than simply label it false.

### Currently unknowable

Use when:

- decisive evidence is non-public;
- a future event has not occurred;
- the source is inaccessible or legitimately confidential;
- available evidence cannot distinguish competing interpretations;
- the proposition is inherently private or not observable from authorised sources.

Do not fill the gap with speculation.

## Confidence calibration

### High confidence

Use when most of the following hold:

- authoritative source fit;
- exact entity and claim match;
- current or period-correct evidence;
- clear wording;
- independent corroboration where incentives warrant it;
- contradiction search completed;
- little unresolved scope ambiguity.

### Medium confidence

Use when:

- the main evidence is credible but incomplete;
- one important corroborating source is missing;
- terminology or scope remains somewhat ambiguous;
- evidence is current enough for the decision but not definitive;
- contradiction search finds unresolved but non-fatal tension.

### Low confidence

Use when:

- only weak, stale, indirect, or partial evidence is available;
- key source records are inaccessible;
- the entity or terminology is ambiguous;
- credible sources materially conflict;
- the claim is changing rapidly.

## Wording discipline

Calibrate verbs and qualifiers to evidence.

Prefer:

- "the official register lists..."
- "the company announced..."
- "the counterparty confirmed..."
- "one study reports..."
- "the available evidence supports..."
- "I could not verify..."
- "the current public evidence does not establish..."

Avoid unless evidence warrants them:

- proves;
- definitely;
- guaranteed;
- has secured;
- approved;
- licensed;
- exclusive;
- industry standard;
- consensus;
- unprecedented.

## Decision-impact calibration

### No material impact

The corrected claim leaves the relevant recommendation or conclusion essentially unchanged.

### Minor qualification

The conclusion remains, but scope, confidence, timing, wording, or a secondary assumption changes.

### Material impact

The evidence meaningfully changes ranking, attractiveness, feasibility, valuation, timing, eligibility, risk, or recommended next step.

### Premise-invalidating impact

A central assumption underlying the recommendation is contradicted or cannot be supported at the required standard. Return the result to the decision owner for re-screen or redesign.

## Common epistemic errors

- treating repeated reporting as independent corroboration when all reports trace to one source;
- treating company silence as proof that something did not occur;
- using current status to infer historical status without period evidence;
- using a study's abstract to support claims not tested in the methods or results;
- interpreting association as causality;
- collapsing intended, announced, approved, contracted, commenced, and completed into the same state;
- treating a database label as equivalent to the underlying legal or commercial status;
- confusing not found with false;
- ignoring a superseding amendment because the older source is easier to locate.

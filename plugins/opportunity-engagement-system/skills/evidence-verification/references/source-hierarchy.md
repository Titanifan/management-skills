# Source Selection and Hierarchy

## Purpose

Choose evidence based on who or what can actually establish the claim. Do not apply a rigid source ranking without considering claim type, incentives, date, and legal or methodological authority.

## General source tiers

### Tier A: authoritative source-fit evidence

Examples:

- legislation, regulation, court or regulator record;
- official register or issuing authority;
- audited filing or exchange filing for reported company facts;
- executed contract or direct record when legitimately available;
- official program guidelines for eligibility, deadlines, and conditions;
- original dataset or study for the specific result it reports;
- current official organisational record for role or status.

Use Tier A as the anchor where it directly answers the question.

### Tier B: strong first-party or high-quality independent evidence

Examples:

- formal company announcement not independently audited;
- named counterparty statement;
- government or institutional media release;
- reputable independent reporting based on direct documents or interviews;
- peer-reviewed research synthesis relevant to the claim.

Tier B can verify many claims, but examine incentives, scope, and whether a stronger authoritative record should exist.

### Tier C: useful secondary evidence

Examples:

- industry databases;
- analyst reports;
- professional commentary;
- reputable trade publications;
- institutional summaries of underlying evidence.

Use for discovery, triangulation, or context. Avoid using Tier C alone for a Critical claim when authoritative evidence should be available.

### Tier D: weak or discovery-only evidence

Examples:

- unsourced aggregators;
- reposts;
- search snippets;
- anonymous social posts;
- promotional summaries without underlying documents;
- AI-generated summaries without inspectable sources.

Do not treat Tier D as verification of a decision-critical claim.

## Claim-specific source fit

| Claim type | Preferred evidence | Important caution |
|---|---|---|
| Licence, approval, registration | Issuer, regulator, official register | Company wording may use licence, approval, qualification, partnership, or certification loosely |
| Listed-company financial fact | Audited report, exchange filing, official results | Separate statutory figures from adjusted metrics and guidance |
| Customer or contract | Executed/public contract, formal filing, named counterparty confirmation | Announcement may omit value, term, exclusivity, conditions, or binding status |
| Funding call deadline or eligibility | Issuing body's guidelines and current program page | Check amendment date and timezone |
| Law or regulation | Current legislation, regulator, official guidance | Commentary may describe an earlier version or another jurisdiction |
| Current job or organisational role | Current official organisation page or direct official record | Cached pages and old bios can be stale |
| Specific research result | Original study and its methods/data | One study does not establish the whole literature |
| General scientific consensus | Systematic review, meta-analysis, major guideline, high-quality synthesis | Check inclusion period, heterogeneity, and recency |
| Market price or live status | Authoritative or high-quality current market data | Timestamp, currency, adjustment, venue, and delay matter |
| News event | Contemporaneous primary record plus strong independent reporting where possible | Early reports often change |
| Historical fact | Primary archival evidence or high-quality scholarly source | Current webpages may simplify history |

## First-party evidence and incentives

Do not reject first-party sources merely because they are interested parties. For many claims they are the only authoritative source.

Instead ask:

- Is the source legally or institutionally accountable for accuracy?
- Is the claim audited, filed, registered, or merely promotional?
- Is the wording precise enough to establish the disputed qualifier?
- Would an independent counterparty or regulator normally confirm it?
- Is omission itself material?

Use corroboration when incentives and ambiguity are both high.

## Corroboration rules

Seek independent corroboration when:

- a Critical claim relies on promotional first-party language;
- the source uses ambiguous commercial terminology;
- a material figure is unaudited or adjusted;
- the event is fast-moving;
- the claim would be unusually consequential if true;
- there is a realistic risk of stale or superseded information.

Do not demand two sources mechanically when one authoritative source is legally definitive.

## Search order

For Critical claims, prefer this order:

1. identify the authority that could establish the fact;
2. retrieve the most current authoritative record;
3. inspect the exact wording, date, version, and scope;
4. search for superseding or contradictory records;
5. use strong independent sources to interpret or corroborate where needed;
6. use weaker sources only for discovery or residual context.

## Negative evidence

Absence of a record is probative only when:

- the relevant register or disclosure system is expected to be complete;
- the search covered the correct entity, aliases, period, and jurisdiction;
- the record would normally be public if the claim were true.

Otherwise classify the claim as Unsupported or Currently unknowable rather than Contradicted.

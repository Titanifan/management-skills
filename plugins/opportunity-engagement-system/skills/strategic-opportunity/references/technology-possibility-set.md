# Technology Possibility-Set Reference

## Purpose

Use this reference when technology may change what strategies, organisational forms, workflows, products, relationships, or policy interventions are feasible, rather than merely making an existing task faster or cheaper.

The central question is not "What can this technology automate?" It is:

**What becomes possible, impossible, newly economical, newly scalable, or newly governable because this technology changes the action set?**

Use this lens to distinguish efficiency gains from genuine changes in strategic possibilities. Treat the resulting possibility map as a hypothesis that requires evidence. Do not infer adoption, demand, value capture, or implementation readiness from technical possibility alone.

This reference is informed by media-effects reasoning associated with Marshall McLuhan and by broader technology-affordance and organisational-change traditions. The executable framework below is an authorial synthesis, not a claim that any single source provides this strategic model.

## 1. Trigger conditions

Load this reference when:

- AI, automation, data, platforms, robotics, digital infrastructure, or another technology is central to the opportunity;
- the user is asking whether a technology creates a new strategic arena rather than a productivity tool;
- a previously infeasible strategy may have become feasible because cost, speed, scale, coordination, information, or customisation changed;
- a technology may shift the bottleneck from technical capability to organisation, governance, distribution, legitimacy, or complementary assets;
- incumbents and entrants may face different possibility sets because they control different data, interfaces, installed bases, capabilities, or relationships;
- a policy or market transition may create a temporary capability window;
- the opportunity claims "transformation" but it is unclear whether the underlying action set has actually changed.

Do not load this reference for routine technology procurement when the strategic possibilities remain unchanged.

## 2. Possibility-set shifts

Check whether the technology changes one or more of the following.

### 2.1 Automation

Can the same task be performed with materially less labour, time, error, or variable cost?

This is an efficiency shift unless it enables a qualitatively new activity, scale, frequency, or business model.

### 2.2 Augmentation

Can humans make decisions, create outputs, perceive patterns, or manage complexity that were previously beyond practical cognitive or analytical limits?

Ask whether augmentation changes decision quality or merely increases output volume.

### 2.3 Coordination

Can more actors, units, suppliers, customers, agents, or data sources coordinate at lower cost or with greater speed?

Coordination shifts can enable new organisational forms, platforms, ecosystem strategies, or distributed operations.

### 2.4 Information visibility

Can previously hidden states, behaviours, risks, or opportunities become observable, auditable, predictable, or traceable?

Visibility may change monitoring, contracting, compliance, pricing, risk allocation, or trust.

### 2.5 Temporal compression

Can sensing, deciding, producing, approving, or responding occur fast enough to make a previously impractical strategy viable?

Faster is strategically meaningful only when timing changes the feasible action set.

### 2.6 Scale and replication

Can an activity be repeated across many users, sites, products, jurisdictions, or decisions with low marginal cost?

Check whether scaling shifts the bottleneck to support, governance, data quality, integration, or distribution.

### 2.7 Personalisation and granularity

Can decisions or offerings be tailored at a level of granularity that was previously uneconomic?

Ask whether the additional granularity creates enough value to justify complexity, privacy, governance, and adoption costs.

### 2.8 Modularity and recombination

Can capabilities be separated, recombined, outsourced, embedded, or exposed through interfaces in new ways?

This can shift where value is created and which layer controls the interface.

### 2.9 Agency and autonomous action

Can software or machines initiate, sequence, monitor, and adapt actions rather than merely provide recommendations?

Autonomy changes governance, accountability, verification, permission, and failure-mode requirements. Do not equate technical agency with safe delegated authority.

### 2.10 New transaction or contracting possibilities

Can technology reduce information asymmetry, verification cost, search cost, coordination cost, or enforcement friction enough to support a transaction that was previously uneconomic?

Check whether institutions, law, trust, or procurement still block the transaction.

## 3. Diagnostic workflow

### Step 1: Establish the pre-technology counterfactual

State how the relevant decision, workflow, product, relationship, or policy problem is handled without the technology.

Identify the strongest real substitute, including manual work, incumbent software, outsourcing, internal teams, or doing nothing.

### Step 2: Identify the changed capability

Specify what technical or economic capability has changed: cost, speed, quality, scale, visibility, coordination, autonomy, personalisation, modularity, or another dimension.

Avoid generic claims such as "AI is more powerful".

### Step 3: Ask whether the action set changes

Identify strategies or activities that were previously:

- technically impossible;
- economically uneconomic;
- too slow;
- too difficult to coordinate;
- too data-intensive;
- too cognitively demanding;
- too expensive to personalise;
- too hard to monitor or verify.

Then ask which of these become feasible now.

### Step 4: Separate substitution from complementarity

Determine whether the technology primarily:

- substitutes for labour, capital, expertise, or an incumbent technology;
- complements existing human, organisational, or institutional capabilities;
- requires entirely new complementary capabilities;
- creates a new layer or interface that reorganises existing complements.

Do not assume substitution is the highest-value path. Complementarity often determines adoption and value capture.

### Step 5: Identify the new bottleneck

If the technology removes one constraint, ask what becomes scarce next.

Typical next bottlenecks include:

- trusted data;
- integration capability;
- domain expertise;
- workflow redesign;
- change management;
- regulation;
- governance;
- verification;
- infrastructure;
- distribution;
- customer trust;
- skilled operators;
- decision rights;
- complementary physical assets.

A strategic opportunity is often located at the new bottleneck rather than in the technology itself.

### Step 6: Identify who gains a different possibility set

Ask whether the same technology creates different possibilities for:

- incumbents versus entrants;
- large versus small firms;
- central versus peripheral units;
- governments versus private firms;
- platform owners versus complementors;
- organisations with proprietary data versus those without it;
- actors with trusted relationships versus actors with only technical capability.

The opportunity may depend on asymmetric access to complements, not the technology's generic capability.

### Step 7: Identify the capability window

Ask why the opportunity exists now.

Check whether technology has advanced faster than:

- organisational capability;
- regulation;
- standards;
- procurement;
- workforce skills;
- infrastructure;
- financing;
- customer understanding;
- governance;
- complementary supply.

A temporary mismatch can create a capability window, but the window can close as complements catch up or standards stabilise.

### Step 8: Test interface control and value capture

Identify the layer where decisions, data, workflow, standards, distribution, or user relationships are controlled.

Ask:

- Who owns the customer or user interface?
- Who controls the workflow position?
- Who owns or governs the critical data?
- Who sets technical or institutional standards?
- Who bears integration cost?
- Who captures savings or new revenue?
- Does the user control a bottleneck, or merely supply a replaceable input?

Technical enablement does not guarantee strategic ownership.

### Step 9: Test second-order organisational effects

Ask what changes because people and organisations adapt to the technology.

Examples:

- decision rights move;
- expertise becomes more or less centralised;
- monitoring expands;
- middle-layer coordination changes;
- new verification roles emerge;
- employees redesign tasks around the tool;
- customers raise expectations;
- regulation responds;
- competitors imitate the feature and shift competition elsewhere.

### Step 10: Translate into the portfolio verdict

State whether the technology:

- merely improves an existing activity;
- creates a meaningful new strategic option;
- opens a new arena;
- shifts the bottleneck to an area where the user has or could build advantage;
- weakens an existing moat;
- creates a temporary window that requires further evidence;
- produces no defensible change after complements and substitutes are considered.

## 4. Complement and bottleneck analysis

For each new possibility, identify the complements required for value realisation.

Use this pattern:

`new technical capability -> newly feasible action -> required complement -> likely bottleneck -> actor controlling bottleneck -> value-capture implication`

Examples of complements include:

- data quality;
- human judgement;
- governance;
- infrastructure;
- standards;
- distribution;
- trusted relationships;
- regulatory approval;
- process redesign;
- procurement;
- financing;
- organisational incentives.

If a complement is missing, do not automatically reject the opportunity. Determine whether the missing complement creates an attractive adjacent opportunity or an unresolvable dependency.

## 5. Strategic value-capture tests

Ask:

- Does the technology create value that is observable to a specific decision owner or user?
- Is the value captured by the technology provider, complementor, customer, platform owner, regulator, or another actor?
- Does repeated use create data, learning, workflow position, trust, distribution, switching cost, or standards influence?
- Is the advantage proprietary, relational, organisational, institutional, or merely based on temporary access to a widely available model or tool?
- Does the technology reduce one cost while commoditising the user's own contribution?
- Could the user occupy an interface, diagnostic, governance, integration, or capability-building position that remains valuable as the underlying technology diffuses?

## 6. Governance and distributional tests

A larger possibility set can include harmful or illegitimate possibilities.

Check:

- accountability and human review;
- privacy and data rights;
- surveillance and power asymmetry;
- safety and reliability;
- labour displacement and work redesign;
- access and inequality;
- regulatory legitimacy;
- procurement and public-value constraints;
- dependence on a platform or infrastructure provider;
- concentration of decision power;
- appeal, audit, and contestability where appropriate.

Do not classify a capability as strategically attractive merely because it is technically feasible.

## 7. Common failure modes

### Old-task substitution trap

The analysis asks only how technology can perform the current task faster.

Correction: ask which previously infeasible actions or organisational forms now become possible.

### Technology determinism

The analysis assumes technical capability produces adoption and impact automatically.

Correction: identify complements, incentives, institutions, workflow integration, and governance.

### Generic-AI moat

The opportunity relies on access to a widely available model or tool.

Correction: identify proprietary data, workflow position, trusted relationships, integration capability, distribution, standards, or another compounding asset.

### Possibility-equals-demand error

A technically feasible action is treated as a market or policy need.

Correction: route demand, sponsor, user, and adoption hypotheses to External Engagement.

### Bottleneck blindness

The analysis celebrates removal of the old constraint but ignores the next one.

Correction: identify the moving bottleneck and who controls it.

### Interface blindness

The user creates technical value but another actor owns the customer, workflow, standard, or data interface.

Correction: assess control and value capture explicitly.

### Permanent-window assumption

The opportunity is treated as durable because technology currently outruns complementary capability.

Correction: ask what happens when skills, regulation, standards, and competitors catch up.

## 9. Routing rules

- If the possibility is technically coherent but external demand, sponsor, buyer, workflow, adoption, or willingness remains unknown, route to External Engagement.
- If the external case is sufficiently formed and the question is whether to commit resources to a prototype, pilot, capability build, integration, or implementation, route to Adaptive Commitment.
- If the issue can be resolved through supplied or public evidence, remain in Strategic Opportunity.
- If the technology claim itself is decision-critical and uncertain, flag it for explicit evidence verification rather than treating technical possibility as fact.

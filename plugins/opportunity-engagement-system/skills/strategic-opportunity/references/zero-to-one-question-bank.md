## 1. Use and attribution

Use this file for a full Zero-to-One stress test after the opportunity passes the rapid screen in `zero-to-one-management-practice.md`.

This question bank follows the complete 14-chapter structure and conclusion of Peter Thiel and Blake Masters's *Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future* (2014). It converts the book's major arguments, contrasts, diagnostics, and examples into decision questions. It is an analytical paraphrase, not a verbatim reproduction or substitute for the book.

The original seven questions remain the scored core. The other chapters supply adversarial tests, score calibration, hard gates, and redesign prompts. Do not count the same idea twice merely because it appears under several chapters.

When applying the framework to public, academic, or management-practice opportunities, explicitly identify any adaptation beyond the book.

## 2. Preface and Chapter 1: The Challenge of the Future

### 2.1 Zero-to-one versus one-to-many

- Is the opportunity creating something genuinely new, or reproducing and distributing an existing model?
- What capability, product, institution, method, or market exists after this opportunity that did not exist before?
- Is the proposed novelty substantive, or only a new label, context, dataset, or interface?
- Could a competent competitor copy the basic idea from public information?
- Does the opportunity represent vertical progress, or mainly horizontal expansion?
- If it is one-to-many rather than zero-to-one, is that acceptable for the user's actual objective?

### 2.2 The contrarian question

- What important claim do capable people in the field largely reject or overlook?
- Can the answer be stated as: most people believe X, but the evidence suggests not-X or a materially different mechanism?
- Is the claim important, falsifiable, and economically or practically consequential?
- Is it genuinely non-consensus, or merely a familiar criticism expressed more aggressively?
- What evidence would prove the contrarian belief wrong?
- Does the opportunity require independent thought, or is it simply anti-consensus imitation?

### 2.3 Valuable and executable novelty

- What is valuable?
- What can this team uniquely do?
- What is nobody else doing, or doing adequately?
- What valuable organisation, product, method, or practice is not being built?
- Does the intersection of value, capability, and neglected opportunity actually exist?
- Is the problem valuable to a user or buyer, not only intellectually interesting?

### 2.4 The company or organisational vehicle

- Is a company, team, centre, platform, partnership, or other bounded organisation the right vehicle?
- Which coordination problems require an organisation rather than isolated contractors or a large bureaucracy?
- Is the proposed organisation small enough to coordinate but large enough to execute?
- Are critical activities being outsourced because the team lacks commitment or capability?
- Can the work be accomplished inside an incumbent institution, or does zero-to-one progress require a new structure?
- What internal and external coordination costs determine the right boundary?

### 2.5 Motivation and learning

- Why undertake this opportunity: status, money, mission, ownership, learning, or world change?
- Would failure produce distinctive knowledge and reusable assets, or only teach how to copy an existing model badly?
- Are non-financial costs, identity effects, and foregone alternatives understood?
- Does the team want the same future strongly enough to endure uncertainty?
- Is the opportunity important enough to justify exceptional effort?

### 2.6 Determinism and exceptionalism

- Is the plan relying on statistical imitation from successful cases, or on a specific causal theory of why this case can work?
- Which elements are path-dependent and cannot be copied from prior winners?
- Is the team confusing unusual ambition with evidence of exceptional ability?
- What must be deliberately designed rather than left to probability?
- How will the team distinguish insight from delusion before irreversible commitment?

## 3. Chapter 2: Party Like It's 1999

### 3.1 Historical learning

- Which historical episode is shaping current conventional wisdom?
- Are current rules based on accurate causal learning or emotional overreaction to a boom, crash, scandal, or failure?
- What did participants believe at the time, and what became clear only later?
- Which apparent lessons are contingent on one era's financing, technology, regulation, or culture?
- Is the team copying the visible practices of past winners while missing the underlying conditions?

### 3.2 The four post-crash dogmas

Stress-test the opportunity against four common rules and their opposites:

- Is incremental improvement safer, or is triviality the greater risk?
- Is staying lean and flexible preserving learning, or avoiding a definite plan?
- Is improving on competitors rational, or does competition destroy differentiation and profit?
- Is product quality enough, or has the team neglected sales and distribution?
- Which side of each contrast is appropriate for this particular opportunity?
- Is the team treating any startup doctrine as universally true?

### 3.3 Bubble and herd thinking

- Is there a widespread, intense, and false belief supporting the opportunity?
- Is the market genuinely in a bubble, or are observers merely repeating a familiar bubble narrative?
- Is the team following the herd, reflexively opposing it, or thinking independently?
- Does the opportunity remain valuable if fashion, subsidies, media attention, or abundant capital disappear?
- Are valuation, user growth, social attention, and real value being confused?
- What specific company or project is valuable, and why, independent of macro slogans?

### 3.4 Growth and business reality

- Does growth improve unit economics, or magnify losses?
- Is customer acquisition creating durable users or purchasing temporary activity?
- Are financing rounds being used to discover the business after the fact?
- What fraud, operational, service, or regulatory problems must be solved before growth becomes real?
- Can the company survive when external capital becomes scarce?

## 4. Chapter 3: All Happy Companies Are Different

### 4.1 Value creation and value capture

- How much value does the opportunity create for users, organisations, or society?
- How much of that value can the organisation capture and reinvest?
- Is high social value paired with structurally weak private value capture?
- Is revenue evidence of value, or merely transfer, subsidy, or temporary arbitrage?
- What mechanism links user benefit to sustainable economics?
- Could another actor capture most of the value created?

### 4.2 Monopoly versus perfect competition

- Is the opportunity unique enough to escape commodity competition?
- Does it solve a specific problem that alternatives do not solve?
- Are margins and economics sufficient to support long-term product, capability, and ecosystem investment?
- Is competition forcing short-term behaviour that prevents meaningful innovation?
- Would success create a category with differentiated value, or only another supplier in a crowded market?

### 4.3 Market-definition honesty

- How would a monopolist exaggerate the breadth of this market to hide dominance?
- How would a weak competitor exaggerate narrowness to claim uniqueness?
- What alternatives does the customer actually consider?
- Which products, services, internal processes, budgets, and non-consumption compete with the opportunity?
- Is the proposed starting market a coherent customer group with shared needs and distribution, or an arbitrary intersection of descriptors?
- What evidence would reveal that the market definition is self-serving?

### 4.4 Unique problem and unique company

- What unique problem is solved?
- Why should this organisation be structurally different from every other participant?
- Which aspect of the business cannot be reduced to a standard industry playbook?
- If all successful businesses are different, what is this opportunity's specific difference?
- Is the differentiation valuable enough to affect purchase, adoption, or outcomes?

## 5. Chapter 4: The Ideology of Competition

### 5.1 Mimetic rivalry

- Is the opportunity attractive because capable peers, universities, firms, or investors are pursuing it?
- Has rivalry narrowed attention to the same prestigious problems, markets, journals, awards, or technologies?
- Is the team competing for status rather than creating new value?
- Would the opportunity still be pursued if competitors were invisible?
- Are competitors determining the roadmap more than customers or first principles?

### 5.2 Competition as self-destruction

- Is rivalry consuming resources that could build a differentiated position?
- Has competition become personal, symbolic, or honour-based?
- Are actors fighting over a shrinking pool because exit feels like defeat?
- Does copying a competitor improve the opportunity or erase its uniqueness?
- What would escaping the contest look like?

### 5.3 Fight, merge, cooperate, or avoid

- Can the rival become a partner, supplier, channel, customer, or acquisition target?
- Would collaboration reduce destructive duplication without sacrificing the secret or control point?
- If conflict is unavoidable, can it be resolved decisively rather than prolonged?
- Which battles are existential, and which are distractions?
- Is the team strong enough to win the necessary fight?

### 5.4 Practical translation

- Is the practice intervention differentiating the organisation or merely helping it imitate a benchmark?
- Does the method reveal uncontested decisions or capabilities?
- Is benchmarking being used as evidence while silently reinforcing sameness?
- What non-rivalrous or category-creating position could replace head-to-head competition?

## 6. Chapter 5: Last Mover Advantage

### 6.1 Long-term value

- What proportion of expected value depends on cash flows or benefits many years in the future?
- Why should users, margins, influence, or strategic control persist?
- Is the current valuation or enthusiasm dominated by near-term growth while long-term economics remain weak?
- What must be true in years 10 to 20 for the opportunity to retain value?
- What would make the business terminally replaceable?

### 6.2 Proprietary technology or method

- Is the core capability materially superior to the nearest substitute?
- Is the advantage large enough to overcome switching cost, trust, habit, and implementation risk?
- Does superiority arise from a breakthrough, integrated design, proprietary data, or a combination competitors cannot easily assemble?
- Is the technology or method independently defensible, or dependent on a third-party platform?
- Can the advantage be demonstrated in the user's terms?

### 6.3 Network effects

- Does each additional user, organisation, case, contributor, or transaction make the product more valuable to others?
- Can the network begin in a small market where early users already benefit?
- Is the claimed network effect genuine, or merely growth, reputation, or data accumulation?
- Are there local, cross-side, data, or standards-based network effects?
- Can the network be multi-homed or easily ported to a competitor?

### 6.4 Economies of scale

- Does growth reduce marginal cost or increase quality disproportionately?
- Can fixed research, data, software, compliance, or distribution costs be spread across users?
- Does scaling increase coordination burden faster than economics improve?
- Is the business model designed for scale, or does every new client require equivalent expert labour?
- Can the market become large enough for scale economies without eroding the beachhead?

### 6.5 Brand

- Does the brand stand for a real and distinctive capability?
- Is brand strength a consequence of substance, or a substitute for it?
- Can trust, legitimacy, and identity reduce adoption friction?
- Would the brand survive if the underlying product were exposed to comparison?
- Is the brand owned by the venture or borrowed from a university, founder, partner, or platform?

### 6.6 Start small and expand deliberately

- What is the smallest market the opportunity can dominate?
- Are customers in the beachhead concentrated, similar, reachable, and underserved?
- Can the team become the default before expanding?
- What adjacent market comes next, and why does the beachhead confer an advantage there?
- Is expansion sequenced, or is the venture simultaneously attacking unrelated markets?
- Does each expansion preserve a coherent secret and control point?

### 6.7 Last mover rather than first mover

- What final important development could this opportunity make in its category?
- Why would later entrants struggle to displace it?
- Is the market still fluid enough to shape but stable enough to retain a position?
- Is the venture entering early merely to be first, without a durable end-state?
- What would make this the last major mover for a meaningful period?

### 6.8 Disruption caution

- Is attacking an incumbent necessary to create value?
- Could the venture begin as a complement or new category rather than invite immediate retaliation?
- Does the language of disruption obscure the need to build a real monopoly?
- What regulatory, competitive, or channel response will the disruption narrative provoke?

## 7. Chapter 6: You Are Not a Lottery Ticket

### 7.1 Definite versus indefinite future

- Does the team believe a better future is possible?
- Is there a specific account of what that future looks like?
- Is the plan designed to create that future, or merely to remain exposed to optionality?
- Are aspirations being mistaken for plans?
- Can the team state what must happen, in what sequence, and by when?

### 7.2 The four future orientations

Use the definite-indefinite and optimistic-pessimistic matrix:

- Is the opportunity definite and optimistic: a better future with a concrete plan?
- Is it indefinite and optimistic: presumed progress without a design?
- Is it definite and pessimistic: a specific plan for managing decline or scarcity?
- Is it indefinite and pessimistic: avoidance without agency?
- Which orientation is embedded in the product, policy, capital allocation, and team behaviour?
- Is that orientation appropriate to the domain?

### 7.3 Planning, design, and iteration

- What is the definite long-term objective?
- Which assumptions require experimentation?
- Is iteration testing a design, or replacing the need to choose one?
- Would a flawed but specific plan reveal more than endless flexibility?
- What is the stopping rule for exploration?
- When must the organisation commit to architecture, standards, customers, or market position?

### 7.4 Optionality and portfolio thinking

- Is optionality creating decision-relevant flexibility or delaying commitment?
- Are many small options preventing concentration on the most valuable path?
- Does the portfolio specify how each option leads to a defined future choice?
- Who bears the cost of keeping options open?
- What opportunity requires closing alternatives to become possible?

### 7.5 Agency versus luck

- Which outcomes can the team control, influence, or only observe?
- Is the founder or organisation treating itself as a lottery ticket?
- How much of the claimed success model depends on favourable markets, policy, timing, or investor sentiment?
- What design choices increase mastery over luck?
- How will the team avoid retrospective stories that confuse fortune with skill?

### 7.6 Creation versus financial manipulation

- Is value being created through technology, organisation, or capability, or mainly rearranged through finance and transactions?
- Does financial engineering support a productive plan or substitute for one?
- Is capital allocated toward a definite future?
- Can the venture explain how money becomes enduring capability and value?

## 8. Chapter 7: Follow the Money

### 8.1 Power-law upside

- Could this opportunity produce an outcome larger than the rest of the relevant portfolio combined?
- Is the upper bound sufficiently large to justify zero-to-one effort?
- What must be true for the tail outcome to occur?
- Is the opportunity merely good, or capable of becoming decisive?
- Does the path have asymmetric upside relative to bounded downside?

### 8.2 Concentration

- Which single market, product, customer segment, channel, capability, or data asset matters most?
- Is one distribution route likely to dominate all others?
- Are resources dispersed because the team cannot form conviction?
- What deserves protected capital and attention rather than equal allocation?
- Which apparently diversified activities are actually competing for the same scarce capability?

### 8.3 What you work on

- Is this the right problem before asking whether it can be executed well?
- Would excellent execution on this opportunity still produce a modest result?
- What alternative opportunity lies further out on the power-law curve?
- Is prestige, familiarity, or sunk cost causing overinvestment in a low-upside path?
- Does the opportunity create the possibility of non-linear learning, impact, ownership, or returns?

### 8.4 Venture and internal portfolio discipline

- Could one investment or project return the entire fund, platform, or career portfolio?
- Is the team pretending every project has similar expected value?
- Are weak projects being retained for diversification despite limited residual assets?
- What evidence justifies concentrating rather than diversifying?
- How will resource allocation change when one path begins to dominate?

## 9. Chapter 8: Secrets

### 9.1 Are secrets still available?

- Does the field assume all important problems are solved or already visible?
- What valuable question remains unanswered because it is difficult, neglected, or institutionally inconvenient?
- Is the opportunity treating a mystery as unknowable when it may contain a discoverable secret?
- What is believed impossible that might only be hard?

### 9.2 Natural secrets

- What does nature, technology, data, or the physical system not yet reveal?
- Which technical relationship, constraint, or capability remains undiscovered?
- Is the secret accessible through a new method, instrument, dataset, experiment, or combination of disciplines?
- What technical proof would establish the secret?
- Can the secret support a proprietary capability rather than a publish-and-copy result?

### 9.3 Human secrets

- What do people, organisations, or institutions know but not state publicly?
- Which incentives prevent action even when actors recognise the problem?
- What is hidden by status, taboo, fear, bureaucracy, fragmented ownership, or strategic silence?
- Which public explanations differ from actual decision criteria?
- Who benefits from keeping the current understanding incomplete?
- Can the venture ethically learn and use this information?

### 9.4 Where to look

- Which disciplines, industries, geographies, user groups, or institutional boundaries are ignored?
- What problem receives little attention because it is not prestigious?
- Where do anomalies persist despite conventional explanations?
- Which important process lacks an owner?
- What can an outsider see that insiders normalise?
- What can an insider see that public data conceal?

### 9.5 Secret quality

- Is the secret true, important, and actionable?
- Does it produce a specific product, method, market, or decision advantage?
- Is it durable enough to build around?
- Can it be independently tested?
- Is the secret merely contrarian rhetoric or a mechanism with evidence?

### 9.6 Sharing and protection

- Who must know the secret for the organisation to execute?
- Who should not know it yet?
- Which part should be published for legitimacy and which part retained for advantage?
- Can confidentiality, data rights, trade secrets, IP, workflow embedding, or speed protect it?
- Will secrecy prevent distribution, collaboration, trust, or scientific validation?

## 10. Chapter 9: Foundations

### 10.1 Founding team

- Do founders or principal collaborators know each other well enough to predict behaviour under pressure?
- Is there shared history rather than a relationship formed solely around the opportunity?
- Are capabilities complementary and responsibilities clear?
- Do founders agree on mission, time horizon, risk, ownership, growth, and exit?
- Are there unresolved conflicts that success will magnify?

### 10.2 Thiel's foundation principle

- Which early choices will become difficult or impossible to reverse?
- Are the venture's legal, governance, equity, IP, data, brand, and employment foundations sound?
- Is the team postponing structural conflict because the opportunity feels urgent?
- What failure mode is being embedded at formation?
- Can redesign remove the defect before build begins?

### 10.3 Ownership, possession, and control

- Who legally and economically owns the venture or asset?
- Who operates it day to day?
- Who has ultimate authority over key decisions?
- Are these three positions aligned?
- Who appoints and removes leaders?
- What happens when owners, operators, and controllers disagree?

### 10.4 Board and governance

- Is the governing group small enough to deliberate and decide?
- Does every board member add essential capability, legitimacy, or oversight?
- Are observers, advisers, and decision makers distinguished?
- Can the board restrain destructive behaviour without paralysing the founder?
- Are reserved matters and escalation paths explicit?

### 10.5 Commitment and employment

- Are core people fully committed, or treating the venture as a side project?
- Is distributed or part-time work compatible with the coordination demands?
- Are key functions internal or outsourced in ways that hollow out capability?
- Does the employment structure create loyalty, learning, and speed?
- Is the team large enough to execute but small enough to remain coherent?

### 10.6 Compensation and incentives

- Do cash compensation, equity, status, and control encourage long-term value creation?
- Is executive pay signalling commitment or extraction?
- Is equity allocation understood as a long-term governance decision rather than a short-term reward?
- Are incentives creating internal comparison and resentment?
- Do contributors who create compounding assets share appropriately in value?

### 10.7 Academic and institutional translation

- Who owns university-created IP, data, code, cases, and brand?
- Are student, collaborator, and institutional contributions governed fairly?
- Can the academic retain publication rights while protecting commercial advantage?
- Are conflict-of-interest and editorial obligations managed?
- Does institutional dependence undermine control or distribution?

## 11. Chapter 10: The Mechanics of Mafia

### 11.1 Why this team?

- Why should exceptional people join this organisation rather than a prestigious incumbent?
- What unique mission can only this team pursue?
- Why are these particular people suited to pursue it together?
- Is the recruiting proposition specific, or generic claims about impact, growth, and talent?
- Would the twentieth high-quality hire find a compelling reason to join?

### 11.2 Culture as substance

- Is culture a set of perks and slogans, or the pattern of who joins, how they work, and what they believe?
- Do team members genuinely like, trust, and respect each other?
- Is the organisation sufficiently distinctive that insiders recognise a shared identity?
- Does the culture improve execution, or merely demand conformity?
- Can disagreement occur without dissolving commitment?

### 11.3 Mission and commitment

- Does the team share a concrete mission beyond compensation?
- Is the mission connected to the secret and product rather than broad social language?
- Are members missionaries, mercenaries, or a workable combination?
- Does commitment persist when external prestige or funding declines?
- Can the mission attract people with scarce capabilities?

### 11.4 Clear roles and internal peace

- Does each key person own one clear primary responsibility?
- Are overlapping domains creating status competition?
- Are evaluation and accountability aligned to role ownership?
- Which internal rivalries could become more destructive than external competition?
- Can leadership redesign roles before factions form?

### 11.5 Cohesion without cult failure

- Is strong internal belief grounded in truth and performance?
- Are outsiders dismissed automatically, or is external evidence taken seriously?
- Can the team preserve unusual conviction without ethical blindness or groupthink?
- What governance mechanisms challenge the mission constructively?
- Is loyalty to the organisation compatible with professional standards and stakeholder duties?

## 12. Chapter 11: If You Build It, Will They Come?

### 12.1 Sales is necessary and often hidden

- Who is responsible for selling, and are they credible?
- Is the team pretending a good product will sell itself?
- Which sales activities are hidden under titles such as business development, partnerships, product, policy, community, or thought leadership?
- Can the founder sell the product, mission, company, and future?
- Does resistance to sales reflect ethics, incompetence, or misunderstanding?

### 12.2 Buyer, user, and decision chain

- Who uses the product?
- Who pays?
- Who controls the budget?
- Who approves procurement, technology, legal, risk, ethics, or policy adoption?
- Who can block implementation?
- Who receives the benefit and who bears switching cost?
- Is the value proposition tailored to each actor?

### 12.3 Distribution economics

- What is the expected customer lifetime value?
- What is the full cost of acquisition, onboarding, implementation, support, and retention?
- Does the gross margin support the chosen channel?
- Is the sales cycle compatible with runway and cash flow?
- Does implementation cost rise linearly with each customer?
- Can the channel scale while preserving trust and quality?

### 12.4 Channel-product fit

- Does the transaction size justify complex founder-led or enterprise sales?
- Can a standard sales team handle the product?
- Is advertising economically sensible?
- Can marketing create demand without expensive persuasion?
- Is product use inherently viral?
- Is the venture in a distribution dead zone between self-service and high-touch sales?
- Which single channel should be mastered first?

### 12.5 Distribution as product design

- Is adoption built into workflow, standards, referrals, data exchange, or collaboration?
- Does the product become easier to sell after each implementation?
- Can users demonstrate value to future users?
- Are partners or institutions necessary for legitimacy and access?
- Could distribution ownership become a stronger moat than product features?

### 12.6 Selling to non-customers

- Can the organisation recruit employees through a compelling story?
- Can it secure investors, partners, regulators, universities, and communities?
- Is the public narrative consistent with the secret and actual business?
- Does the founder communicate differently to each audience without becoming incoherent?
- Are reputation and relationship channels intentionally built?

## 13. Chapter 12: Man and Machine

### 13.1 Complementarity rather than substitution

- What do humans do uniquely well in this workflow?
- What do machines do uniquely well?
- Can the combination produce a result substantially better than either alone?
- Is the technology designed to augment judgement, coordination, creativity, empathy, or accountability?
- Is the venture unnecessarily promising full automation when a complementary system is more valuable?

### 13.2 New capabilities and tasks

- Does the technology enable work that was previously impossible?
- Does it create new roles, decisions, markets, or services rather than only remove labour?
- Which human capabilities become more valuable after adoption?
- What training, role redesign, and governance are required?
- Does the organisation capture the complementarity, or does it accrue mainly to a platform supplier?

### 13.3 Data and interface design

- What data can machines process that humans cannot manage at scale?
- What context, tacit knowledge, values, and exceptions require human judgement?
- Where should human review, appeal, override, and accountability sit?
- Does feedback from human use improve the system?
- Can that feedback become a proprietary data or workflow advantage?
- Is the interface designed around decisions rather than technical outputs?

### 13.4 Automation risk and legitimacy

- What errors become more frequent, less visible, or more consequential after automation?
- Are users able to understand and challenge the system?
- Could automation intensify surveillance, unsafe work, bias, exclusion, deskilling, or responsibility gaps?
- Does the human-machine system remain auditable?
- Can the venture earn trust from workers, customers, regulators, and affected communities?

### 13.5 Management-practice opportunity

- What management problem exists because technology adoption is treated as a technical rather than organisational system?
- Can the practice intervention improve task-technology fit, workflow redesign, capability, governance, or value capture?
- Is the true zero-to-one insight located in the complementarity architecture rather than the underlying technology?

## 14. Chapter 13: Seeing Green and the Original Seven Questions

### 14.1 Macro importance is not a business

- Is the opportunity justified mainly because the sector is large, urgent, subsidised, strategic, or socially important?
- What micro-level plan allows the organisation to benefit from the macro insight?
- Does a large social need translate into a customer, buyer, budget, and value-capture mechanism?
- Is the team merely participating in an important sector, or building a unique company?
- Would the opportunity remain attractive if the broad sector narrative weakened?

### 14.2 Social entrepreneurship and difference

- Is the organisation combining the weaknesses of nonprofit and commercial models while claiming both virtues?
- What does the intervention do differently for society, not merely more of the same agenda?
- Can public value and private value capture coexist without mission drift?
- Is social impact a measurable mechanism or a branding category?
- Does the venture create net value after negative externalities and opportunity costs?

### 14.3 The original seven-question dashboard

Preserve these seven named questions in every full venture assessment:

1. **Engineering:** Is the solution a breakthrough or step-change rather than a marginal improvement?
2. **Timing:** Why is this the right moment to start?
3. **Monopoly:** Can the venture begin with a large share of a small, real market?
4. **People:** Is the team right for the mission, technology, market, and governance demands?
5. **Distribution:** Can the product or practice be delivered, sold, adopted, and repeated?
6. **Durability:** Can the market position remain defensible for 10 to 20 years?
7. **Secret:** Has the team identified a valuable opportunity that others do not see?

Do not treat five or six superficial yes answers as sufficient. Require evidence, score caps, and a plan to resolve weak dimensions.

### 14.4 Engineering stress test

- Is the improvement large enough to overcome incumbent scale, trust, and switching cost?
- Does integrated execution produce superiority even when individual components are available to others?
- Is the technology commercially and operationally ready?
- Is the venture relying on incremental cost decline that all competitors share?

### 14.5 Timing stress test

- Does a policy, cost, infrastructure, social, or financing window create a temporary advantage?
- Can the venture survive after subsidies or favourable capital conditions change?
- Are complementary technologies and standards ready?
- Is the opportunity entering a slow market with a definite takeover plan?

### 14.6 Monopoly stress test

- Is the market genuinely narrow, or is the team ignoring broader substitutes?
- Can the venture dominate a beachhead before global or commodity competition arrives?
- Does sector structure allow differentiation and value capture?
- Will powerful suppliers, platforms, states, or customers appropriate the advantage?

### 14.7 People stress test

- Does the team contain actual technical and operational capability, or mainly finance, policy, and presentation skill?
- Can the team integrate engineering, manufacturing, sales, regulation, and capital?
- Are founders and executives credible to scarce technical talent and customers?
- Is mission competence being confused with mission enthusiasm?

### 14.8 Distribution stress test

- Can customers buy, install, integrate, finance, and operate the product?
- Who coordinates infrastructure, permitting, standards, service, and complementary assets?
- Is the venture assuming that someone else will solve adoption?
- Are distribution and implementation designed at the same time as technology?

### 14.9 Durability stress test

- What substitute technology, commodity price, policy change, geopolitical shift, or manufacturing scale could erase the advantage?
- Is the cost or performance advantage an order of magnitude or only temporary?
- Can the venture retain learning and IP as manufacturing and supply chains scale?
- What prevents a lower-cost competitor from commoditising the market?

### 14.10 Secret stress test

- Is the opportunity based on a distinctive technological or behavioural insight?
- Is the team repeating a broad convention such as green demand will grow or AI will transform industry?
- What underexplored mechanism explains why this venture succeeds where the sector has failed?
- Does the secret survive contact with technical, customer, and policy evidence?

## 15. Chapter 14: The Founder's Paradox

### 15.1 Extreme traits

- Which unusual founder traits create vision, persuasion, speed, or endurance?
- Do the same traits create governance, ethical, interpersonal, or execution risk?
- Is apparent eccentricity authentic, cultivated, or used to excuse poor behaviour?
- Can the team distinguish productive nonconformity from instability or self-deception?

### 15.2 Insider and outsider status

- Does the founder understand the field deeply while retaining an outsider's perspective?
- Can the founder cross boundaries that established actors cannot?
- Is outsider status producing a secret, or merely ignorance of constraints?
- What legitimacy must be built with insiders, regulators, experts, and communities?

### 15.3 Founder centrality

- Is a strong founder necessary to hold the secret, mission, and design together?
- Can the founder bring out exceptional work from others?
- Is the organisation becoming dependent on one person's decisions, relationships, or charisma?
- Which functions must be institutionalised before dependence becomes dangerous?
- Can leadership succession preserve the mission without preserving every founder habit?

### 15.4 Myth, fame, and scapegoating

- Is the organisation constructing a founder myth that distorts learning or accountability?
- Are successes attributed entirely to the founder and failures displaced onto others?
- Could public prominence create regulatory, reputation, key-person, or governance risk?
- Is the founder able to share credit and accept constraint?
- How will the organisation prevent adulation from suppressing evidence?

### 15.5 Governance balance

- Can governance constrain destructive extremes without turning the company into a conventional bureaucracy?
- Are there trusted people able to challenge the founder?
- Which decisions require founder control and which require independent oversight?
- Does the board understand the secret well enough to govern without flattening it?
- What happens if the founder becomes unavailable, unwilling, or misaligned?

### 15.6 Do not overestimate the individual

- Is the founder confusing centrality with sole value creation?
- Does the venture depend on complementary team, users, institutions, infrastructure, and timing?
- Can the founder create conditions in which others perform at their best?
- Is ownership proportional to contribution, risk, and long-term responsibility?

## 16. Conclusion: Stagnation or Singularity?

### 16.1 Future-state test

- Which future does the opportunity make more likely: stagnation, recurrent collapse, extinction, or technological takeoff?
- Does it create genuine progress or only redistribute existing value?
- Can it address constraints that horizontal growth intensifies?
- Does it extend zero-to-one progress beyond fashionable digital applications?
- Is the proposed future compatible with human flourishing and institutional legitimacy?

### 16.2 Agency and responsibility

- What must people deliberately build for the better future to occur?
- Which outcomes will not happen automatically through markets, policy, or technology?
- Does the organisation take responsibility for negative externalities and systemic risk?
- Can it maintain optimism without assuming inevitability?
- What irreversible choices should be made cautiously?

### 16.3 Management-practice translation

- Does the practice help organisations build a better future, or only adapt to present constraints?
- Can it turn technological possibility into organisational capability and legitimate action?
- Is the intervention enabling creation, coordination, and ownership rather than only commentary?
- What enduring institution, capability, or decision system remains if the project succeeds?

### Final adversarial questions

Before issuing a high ZVP verdict, answer all of the following:

- What is the non-consensus secret, and what would falsify it?
- What is genuinely zero-to-one rather than a new wrapper around one-to-many work?
- Why now?
- What exact small market can be dominated?
- What real alternatives compete for the same budget and workflow?
- Who buys, who uses, and which channel can dominate?
- Why is the team structurally able to win?
- How are ownership, possession, and control aligned?
- Which monopoly mechanisms strengthen with scale?
- What makes the business durable for 10 to 20 years?
- What single factor lies on the power-law tail?
- Is there a definite plan rather than indefinite optionality?
- Can value detach from personal labour and become an ownable compounding asset?
- What future does success create, and who bears its downside?

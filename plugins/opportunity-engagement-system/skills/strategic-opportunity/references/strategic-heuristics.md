# Strategic Heuristics for Opportunity Selection

## Purpose

Use this reference when a strategic opportunity must be judged under uncertainty, competing constraints, limited attention, path dependence, or a need to sequence actions rather than optimise all dimensions simultaneously.

Use the heuristics as disciplined prompts, not as universal laws. They are designed to improve selection quality by forcing explicit decomposition, prioritisation, sequencing, and learning. They do not authorise adversarial, zero-sum, political, military, or coercive assumptions in settings where those assumptions are inappropriate.

This reference combines first-principles decomposition with selected strategic heuristics drawn from practice-oriented traditions, including ideas associated with contradiction analysis, investigation before judgement, concentration of effort, sequencing, and learning through action. The executable workflow is an authorial synthesis for Strategic Opportunity, not a reproduction of any single source.

## 1. Trigger conditions

Load this reference when the strategic verdict depends materially on one or more of the following:

- the opportunity contains many apparent problems but one or two may be decisive;
- the user must choose among competing objectives, projects, or arenas;
- resources, attention, access, timing, or institutional support are constrained;
- the opportunity requires a sequence rather than one irreversible decision;
- the external situation is changing and the correct response may depend on timing;
- bounded action could generate information that desk analysis cannot provide;
- success depends on concentrating effort at a leverage point rather than spreading effort broadly;
- the opportunity could be misjudged because attractive labels obscure the concrete situation;
- the user is at risk of importing a generic playbook into a context with different actors, incentives, institutions, or power relationships.

Do not load this reference merely because a decision is important. Use it when decomposition, prioritisation, concentration, sequencing, or action-learning could materially change the portfolio verdict.

## 2. Core heuristics

### 2.1 Start from the concrete situation

Do not begin with a preferred framework, fashionable label, or analogy.

Identify:

- the actual decision;
- the actors and institutions that shape it;
- the current constraints;
- the time horizon;
- what has changed recently;
- what is known, inferred, and unknown;
- which assumptions are imported from another context.

Treat a framework as a lens to interrogate the situation, not as the situation itself.

### 2.2 Decompose to first principles

For any attractive claim, ask what must be true for it to hold.

Break the opportunity into:

- desired outcome;
- necessary conditions;
- sufficient conditions where identifiable;
- constraints;
- causal or value-realisation mechanism;
- complements;
- dependencies;
- failure conditions.

Then distinguish:

- facts already evidenced;
- assumptions that can be checked from existing evidence;
- field hypotheses that require External Engagement;
- resource-backed tests that belong to Adaptive Commitment.

Do not use "first principles" as permission to ignore accumulated evidence, domain knowledge, institutions, or history.

### 2.3 Find the primary contradiction or binding strategic tension

Many opportunities contain several tensions. Identify the one most likely to govern the strategic verdict now.

Examples include:

- demand exists but distribution is absent;
- policy support exists but implementation capability is scarce;
- technology works but organisational adoption does not;
- data exists but ownership or legitimacy is weak;
- a partner is enthusiastic but the user's role is peripheral;
- the research question is strong but the evidence pathway is fragile;
- market growth is attractive but value capture is structurally weak.

State the governing tension in one sentence.

Do not assume the same tension remains primary over time. If the constraint is relieved, identify what is likely to become primary next.

### 2.4 Distinguish primary from secondary constraints

Do not allocate equal analytical weight to every weakness.

Classify constraints as:

- **binding now:** currently prevents the opportunity from becoming viable or strategically attractive;
- **next-order:** likely to bind after the current constraint is relieved;
- **important but non-binding:** affects quality but does not govern the current verdict;
- **noise or distraction:** visible but unlikely to change the decision.

A long problem list is not a diagnosis.

### 2.5 Concentrate effort where it changes the decision

Prefer a small number of decisive questions, assets, relationships, or evidence points over broad but shallow activity.

Ask:

- Which missing fact could change the disposition?
- Which actor controls the critical access, authority, or resource?
- Which capability would unlock several downstream possibilities?
- Which asset would compound across multiple opportunities?
- Which bottleneck, if relieved, creates the largest increase in option value?

Do not confuse concentration with overcommitment. Strategic Opportunity identifies where concentration would matter; Adaptive Commitment decides how much resource to commit.

### 2.6 Seek local advantage before broad expansion

When the opportunity is too broad, identify a narrower domain in which the user's access, method, evidence, reputation, or relationship position is unusually strong.

A strong local position can provide:

- proprietary learning;
- credibility;
- reference cases;
- data;
- distribution;
- repeat relationships;
- a platform for adjacent expansion.

Reject artificial narrowing that merely hides real substitutes or competition.

### 2.7 Sequence before scaling

Ask what must be learned or established before the next irreversible step.

A useful sequence should:

- resolve the most decision-relevant uncertainty early;
- avoid building expensive complements before the core thesis is credible;
- preserve reversibility where uncertainty is high;
- create reusable assets even if the opportunity is later abandoned;
- make the next decision easier, not merely postpone it.

Strategic Opportunity may identify the strategic sequence. Adaptive Commitment owns milestones, resource caps, persistence periods, and execution contracts.

### 2.8 Use action to learn when observation is insufficient

Some strategic questions cannot be answered from desk research alone because the opportunity is partly created through interaction, experimentation, or commitment.

Ask whether a bounded action could reveal:

- genuine demand;
- counterpart behaviour;
- access quality;
- capability requirements;
- implementation friction;
- willingness to commit resources;
- hidden constraints.

Route relationship- or field-learning actions to External Engagement. Route material resource-backed tests to Adaptive Commitment.

Do not rationalise activity as learning unless the action has an explicit belief it could update.

### 2.9 Revise after practice, not after emotion

Update the strategic judgement when material evidence changes, not merely because the user feels more invested, more excited, more anxious, or more familiar with the opportunity.

Record:

- the prior belief;
- the new evidence;
- what assumption changed;
- whether the primary constraint changed;
- whether the portfolio disposition changed.

Do not let sunk effort or relationship warmth substitute for evidence.

### 2.10 Test whether the situation is actually adversarial

Some strategic traditions emerge from conflict settings. Before borrowing a competitive or adversarial heuristic, ask:

- Are actors truly competing for a fixed resource?
- Can cooperation expand the available value?
- Is legitimacy more important than domination?
- Does the user depend on long-run reciprocal relationships?
- Could aggressive concentration, secrecy, or positioning damage trust or public-interest obligations?

Use zero-sum logic only when the structure is genuinely zero-sum or strongly rivalrous.

### 2.11 Preserve ethical and institutional constraints

Do not treat legal, ethical, public-interest, authorship, governance, confidentiality, procurement, or stakeholder constraints as obstacles to be "outmanoeuvred".

Treat legitimate constraints as part of the strategic problem definition.

## 3. Strategic diagnostic workflow

Use this sequence only when the heuristics could materially change selection.

### Step 1: State the concrete decision

Express the immediate strategic question in one sentence.

### Step 2: Decompose the opportunity

List the minimum conditions required for the claimed value to exist.

### Step 3: Identify the governing tension

Name the primary contradiction or binding strategic tension.

### Step 4: Rank constraints

Separate binding-now, next-order, important-non-binding, and distracting issues.

### Step 5: Identify concentration points

Name the one to three evidence points, actors, capabilities, or assets where additional attention would most change the verdict.

### Step 6: Test local advantage

Ask whether a narrower starting position would create a stronger and more defensible pathway.

### Step 7: Identify the strategic sequence

State what logically must precede what. Do not set detailed milestones or budgets.

### Step 8: Decide whether evidence must come from desk research, field interaction, or resource-backed action

Route accordingly.

### Step 9: Run the non-zero-sum and legitimacy check

Remove conflict assumptions that do not fit the context.

### Step 10: Translate into selection consequences

State which finding raises, lowers, redesigns, or leaves unchanged the portfolio verdict.

## 4. Boundary translation rules

Use the following translations cautiously:

- **Investigation before judgement** -> verify the concrete situation before applying a generic strategy.
- **Primary contradiction** -> identify the governing strategic tension or binding constraint, not a political doctrine.
- **Concentration of force** -> concentrate scarce attention, evidence gathering, capability building, or relationship effort at decisive points, not coercion.
- **Local superiority** -> seek a defensible beachhead or domain-specific advantage before broad expansion.
- **Practice and revision** -> use evidence-producing action to update beliefs when observation is insufficient.
- **Strategic initiative** -> shape timing, relationships, standards, capabilities, or option sets when feasible, without assuming control over all external actors.

Do not preserve militarised language in user-facing output unless the user explicitly requests historical comparison.

## 5. Common failure modes

### Framework-first reasoning

The analysis starts with a favourite concept and forces the opportunity into it.

Correction: restate the concrete situation without theoretical labels.

### Everything-is-primary reasoning

Every weakness is described as decisive.

Correction: identify what actually governs the current verdict and what would bind next.

### Resource spreading

The user pursues many weak signals because each looks potentially valuable.

Correction: identify which opportunities or evidence points can create cumulative assets and which merely add activity.

### Premature confrontation

A competitive metaphor makes cooperative, institutional, or public-interest relationships look adversarial.

Correction: test whether value can be jointly created and whether legitimacy or reciprocity is central.

### Action without learning

The user takes meetings, writes proposals, or runs pilots without a belief-update objective.

Correction: state what evidence the action must produce before proceeding.

### Permanent provisionality

Sequencing becomes an excuse never to commit.

Correction: once strategic and field evidence are adequate, hand off to Adaptive Commitment for a bounded commitment contract.

## 7. Routing rules

- If the decisive unknown concerns the real external problem, actor, sponsor, buyer, access, reciprocity, ownership, or willingness, route to External Engagement.
- If the opportunity is sufficiently formed and the decisive next move requires material time, money, protected attention, staff, prototype, pilot, or implementation, route to Adaptive Commitment.
- If the uncertainty can be resolved from supplied or public evidence, remain in Strategic Opportunity.
- If the heuristic reveals a strategic hard gate, redesign, monitor, service-only, or reject without using further activity to rescue the opportunity.

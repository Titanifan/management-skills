# Evidence Base for External Engagement

## Purpose and limits

This reference summarises the research informing the skill. It supports the workflow but does not claim that every rule is directly tested in every sector or country.

Distinguish:

- **research-supported propositions:** recurring findings from university–industry, knowledge-transfer, intermediary, policy-engagement, and academic-career research;
- **managerial synthesis:** practical rules derived from those findings for the user's portfolio;
- **local institutional requirements:** promotion, contracting, conflicts, procurement, IP, confidentiality, lobbying, and research-governance rules that must be checked separately.

The phrase **“borrow the introduction; build the direct relationship”** is a managerial synthesis, not a quotation from a source.

## Core propositions

### 1. Individual academics and principal investigators matter

D'Este and Patel find that individual researcher characteristics have a stronger influence on the variety and frequency of industry interaction than departmental or university characteristics. Boehm and Hogan find that principal investigators play a lead role in establishing and managing collaborative networks and act as project managers, negotiators, resource acquirers, and boundary spanners.

Implication:

- institutional access is useful but does not replace the user's own direct relationship and visible leadership;
- the user should become the recognised academic counterpart rather than remain peripheral labour inside another person's network.

Sources:

- D'Este, P., & Patel, P. (2007). University–industry linkages in the UK: What are the factors underlying the variety of interactions with industry? *Research Policy, 36*(9), 1295–1313. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2007.05.002
- Boehm, D. N., & Hogan, T. (2014). ‘A jack of all trades’: The role of PIs in the establishment and management of collaborative networks in scientific knowledge commercialisation. *The Journal of Technology Transfer, 39*(1), 134–149. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10961-012-9273-8
- Boehm, D. N., & Hogan, T. (2013). Science-to-Business collaborations: A science-to-business marketing perspective on scientific knowledge commercialization. *Industrial Marketing Management, 42*(4), 564–579. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.indmarman.2012.12.001

### 2. Personal and institutional governance modes coexist

Bodas Freitas, Geuna, and Rossi distinguish university-mediated institutional interactions from formal personal interactions between firms and individual academics. Their findings indicate that personal contractual interactions are substantial and complementary to institutional channels.

Implication:

- a university centre, colleague, advisory board, or broker can open the door;
- the subsequent relationship should develop direct user–counterpart communication and clear attribution, subject to university governance.

Source:

- Bodas Freitas, I. M., Geuna, A., & Rossi, F. (2013). Finding the right partners: Institutional and personal modes of governance of university–industry interactions. *Research Policy, 42*(1), 50–62. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2012.06.007

### 3. Trust and prior experience reduce collaboration barriers

Bruneel, D'Este, and Salter find that prior collaborative experience reduces orientation-related barriers and inter-organisational trust reduces both orientation- and transaction-related barriers. Rybnicek and Königsgruber's systematic review identifies institutional, relationship, output, and framework factors affecting collaboration success.

Implication:

- use bounded, reciprocal interactions to build trust;
- do not infer that trust requires repeated free delivery;
- as the relationship matures, role clarity, commitment, resources, and governance should become more explicit.

Sources:

- Bruneel, J., D'Este, P., & Salter, A. (2010). Investigating the factors that diminish the barriers to university–industry collaboration. *Research Policy, 39*(7), 858–868. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2010.03.006
- Rybnicek, R., & Königsgruber, R. (2019). What makes industry–university collaboration succeed? A systematic review of the literature. *Journal of Business Economics, 89*, 221–250. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11573-018-0916-6

### 4. Intermediaries can reduce search and translation costs

Howells synthesises the roles of innovation intermediaries, including connecting, brokering, searching, translating, and supporting innovation processes.

Implication:

- intermediaries such as growth centres, chambers, adoption centres, research-translation organisations, and industry associations can be efficient access routes;
- their value should be tested by whether they produce named introductions, problem intelligence, decision access, or funding pathways;
- mediated access should ideally become a direct substantive relationship.

Source:

- Howells, J. (2006). Intermediation and the role of intermediaries in innovation. *Research Policy, 35*(5), 715–728. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2006.03.005

### 5. Academic engagement is broader than commercialisation

Perkmann and colleagues define academic engagement to include collaborative research, contract research, consulting, and informal interactions. Their 2013 review notes that engagement is often aligned with traditional research and used to access resources supporting research agendas. Their 2021 review reports a generally positive association between academic engagement and research productivity.

Implication:

- success should not be restricted to patents or ventures;
- engagement should ideally generate research, data, funding, methods, policy, reputation, or relationships that compound;
- activity without identifiable assets should be downgraded.

Sources:

- Perkmann, M., Tartari, V., McKelvey, M., Autio, E., Broström, A., D'Este, P., Fini, R., Geuna, A., Grimaldi, R., Hughes, A., Krabel, S., Kitson, M., Llerena, P., Lissoni, F., Salter, A., & Sobrero, M. (2013). Academic engagement and commercialisation: A review of the literature on university–industry relations. *Research Policy, 42*(2), 423–442. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2012.09.007
- Perkmann, M., Salandra, R., Tartari, V., McKelvey, M., & Hughes, A. (2021). Academic engagement: A review of the literature 2011–2019. *Research Policy, 50*(1), 104114. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2020.104114

### 6. Teaching-focused collaboration is distinct from research conversion

Teaching-focused university–industry collaboration can improve student competencies and has legitimate educational value. It is analytically distinct from contract research, consulting, sponsored research, or personally owned external relationships.

Implication:

- classify pro-bono student projects accurately as teaching/WIL unless evidence shows further conversion;
- do not use repeated semesters as a proxy for willingness to fund research.

Source:

- Borah, D., Malik, K., & Massini, S. (2021). Teaching-focused university–industry collaborations: Determinants and impact on graduates' employability competencies. *Research Policy, 50*(3), 104172. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2020.104172

### 7. Industry and firm context varies; sector wealth is not a sufficient rule

Cohen, Nelson, and Walsh find that public research affects industrial R&D through multiple channels and that influence varies across industries and firms. Meyer-Krahmer and Schmoch show that university–industry interaction patterns differ across technological fields.

Implication:

- use sector resources as a prior, not a hard gate;
- screen for internal capability, senior sponsorship, absorptive capacity, persistent problem, and resource commitment;
- large firms, start-ups, and smaller organisations may each be valuable for different reasons.

Sources:

- Cohen, W. M., Nelson, R. R., & Walsh, J. P. (2002). Links and impacts: The influence of public research on industrial R&D. *Management Science, 48*(1), 1–23. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.48.1.1.14273
- Meyer-Krahmer, F., & Schmoch, U. (1998). Science-based technologies: University–industry interactions in four fields. *Research Policy, 27*(8), 835–851. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0048-7333(98)00094-8

### 8. Policy engagement is relational, role-sensitive, and costly

Oliver and Cairney's systematic review of advice to academics emphasises high-quality and relevant research, understanding policy processes, accessibility, relationships, ground rules, and a deliberate choice between advocacy and honest brokerage. It also warns that policy engagement can be labour-intensive, uncertain, and unevenly rewarded.

Implication:

- treat policy engagement as a process rather than a one-off submission;
- identify the policy stage, decision authority, role, independence, and follow-up channel;
- do not assume that more engagement automatically creates impact;
- protect attention, safety, legitimacy, and conflicts.

Source:

- Oliver, K., & Cairney, P. (2019). The dos and don'ts of influencing policy: A systematic review of advice to academics. *Palgrave Communications, 5*, 21. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-019-0232-y

### 9. Engagement can be difficult to recognise in promotion

Smith, Else, and Crookes review engagement and academic promotion in the Australian context and note that engaged work can be difficult to define, measure, report, and reward in systems emphasising publications and grant income. Jensen and colleagues find that scientists active in wider dissemination also perform strongly academically, while dissemination itself has little direct career effect in their setting.

Implication:

- document initiation, leadership, resources, outputs, use, and attributable impact;
- convert engagement into recognised assets where appropriate;
- do not assume that participation or relationship proximity will be self-evidently rewarded;
- check the user's institution-specific promotion rules separately.

Sources:

- Smith, K. M., Else, F., & Crookes, P. A. (2014). Engagement and academic promotion: A review of the literature. *Higher Education Research & Development, 33*(4), 836–847. https://doi.org/10.1080/07294360.2013.863849
- Jensen, P., Rouquier, J.-B., Kreimer, P., & Croissant, Y. (2008). Scientists who engage with society perform better academically. *Science and Public Policy, 35*(7), 527–541. https://doi.org/10.3152/030234208X329130

## Managerial rules derived from the evidence

The following rules are practical syntheses rather than direct estimates from a single study:

1. Seek a direct relationship with the problem owner and sponsor after a warm or brokered introduction.
2. Do not treat long relationship duration or repeated student projects as evidence of research conversion.
3. Permit bounded free scoping but require reciprocal resources before substantive delivery.
4. Identify both the person who understands the problem and the person who can mobilise resources or authority.
5. Treat a node as strategically relevant when it is a credible payer/resource owner, gateway/influence node, or both.
6. Select funding mechanisms after the problem and relationship are credible.
7. Build attributable outputs and evidence trails to make external engagement legible for career evaluation.
8. For policy work, protect independence and distinguish public contribution from private advocacy.

## Calibration cautions

- University contracting, IP, conflicts, outside-work, and promotion rules vary.
- Many studies are observational; avoid causal overclaiming.
- Science and engineering dominate parts of the university–industry literature; adapt mechanisms carefully for business, policy, social science, and community settings.
- “Ownership” should not mean exclusion, appropriation of a colleague's relationship, or circumvention of institutional obligations.
- Payment is not the only valid outcome, particularly in public-policy and community engagement.
- A famous or wealthy organisation is not automatically resource-ready.
- A small organisation is not automatically low-value if it supplies authority, data, implementation access, or gateway value.

# Policy and Public Engagement Track

## Purpose

Use this reference for regulators, government departments, public agencies, consultations, parliamentary or administrative processes, industry associations acting in a policy capacity, community organisations, public-interest networks, and public-facing expert engagement.

The objective is to create legitimate, independent, and attributable influence on a real policy or public decision while building durable relationships and evidence assets.

## Define the engagement position

State which role the user is taking:

- **Independent expert:** explains evidence, mechanisms, trade-offs, and uncertainty.
- **Honest broker:** clarifies multiple defensible options and their consequences.
- **Issue advocate:** openly argues for a defined public-interest position.
- **Research partner:** co-produces evidence with policymakers or affected stakeholders.
- **Evaluator:** assesses implementation, outcomes, or unintended effects.
- **Convenor:** brings together actors with different knowledge or interests.
- **Private adviser:** provides bounded advice under an explicit mandate.

Do not blur independent expertise with undisclosed advocacy for a private organisation.

## Policy-process diagnosis

Identify:

- the exact policy decision, rule, standard, funding allocation, implementation process, or public problem;
- the formal and informal decision owners;
- the current policy stage: agenda setting, consultation, design, authorisation, implementation, evaluation, or revision;
- the policy window and deadline;
- evidence already in circulation;
- stakeholder coalitions, opposition, and distributional effects;
- which claims are technically contestable or politically constrained;
- what decision could realistically change.

A public consultation is not automatically a high-impact opportunity. Determine whether submissions are read, how they enter the process, and what follow-up channels exist.

## Policy authority map

Distinguish:

- formal decision authority;
- policy officers and analysts;
- ministerial or executive advisers;
- regulator or statutory body;
- implementation agency;
- treasury or funding authority;
- industry and community stakeholders;
- expert committees;
- public or media audience.

The most visible person is not always the decision owner. The most knowledgeable officer is not always authorised to act.

## Policy qualification questions

### Relevance

- What live policy or implementation decision needs evidence?
- Why is the issue salient now?
- What would happen without intervention?
- Which evidence gap is genuinely decision-relevant?

### Access

- Is there a formal consultation, briefing, roundtable, committee, or advisory pathway?
- Who can sponsor access to the relevant officials or stakeholders?
- Can the engagement continue after submission or publication?

### Independence

- Who benefits from the position?
- Are interests and affiliations disclosed?
- Is the user being asked to advocate for a firm's preferred regulatory treatment?
- Can the user publish or state conclusions independently?
- Is the user able to discuss uncertainty, trade-offs, and affected groups?

### Impact pathway

- Which decision, wording, process, guidance, allocation, or implementation practice might change?
- Can the influence be traced through citations, meetings, invitations, draft changes, implementation decisions, or public acknowledgement?
- What follow-up evidence would be needed?

### Ownership and attribution

- Is the user the author, lead expert, named contributor, or convenor?
- Will the contribution be publicly recorded or otherwise documentable?
- Does the engagement build a recognisable policy identity or durable relationship?

## Public-policy engagement sequence

1. Verify the policy process and current stage.
2. Identify decision authority and relevant officials.
3. Determine the user's role: expert, honest broker, advocate, researcher, evaluator, or convenor.
4. Diagnose the evidence need and policy friction.
5. Map stakeholders and distributional consequences.
6. Decide whether a public submission, private briefing, roundtable, research project, or implementation evaluation is the right mechanism.
7. Protect independence, disclosure, confidentiality, and conflict boundaries.
8. Produce a proportionate contribution.
9. Seek a follow-up relationship, not only a one-off document.
10. Record attributable use and impact without overclaiming causality.

## Public credibility as an access route

Use prior submissions, publications, public commentary, or expert talks to establish that the user has already engaged seriously with the problem.

Do not present public work as a sales brochure. Use it to justify a substantive question such as:

- how the issue appears from the organisation's operational perspective;
- which implementation friction is missing from the public debate;
- what evidence would help regulators or industry make a better decision;
- where policy design creates unintended organisational consequences.

## Engagement with firms around policy

When speaking with a company about regulation:

- seek factual operational insight and multiple perspectives;
- do not promise to advocate the company's desired outcome;
- disclose relevant relationships when making public submissions;
- maintain the ability to disagree;
- distinguish commissioned advice from independent public scholarship;
- avoid using confidential information in public work without permission;
- assess whether later consultancy would create a conflict with prior or ongoing public roles.

## Resource and commitment signals in policy work

Payment is only one signal. Also consider:

- formal invitation to brief decision-makers;
- access to policy staff or implementation data;
- inclusion in an expert or advisory process;
- commissioning of analysis or evaluation;
- agency or stakeholder staff time;
- public citation or acknowledgement;
- implementation access;
- repeat invitations;
- referral to another decision process;
- support for a research grant, fellowship, or data partnership.

## Appropriate mechanisms

- public consultation submission;
- regulatory or parliamentary evidence;
- private policy briefing;
- expert roundtable or workshop;
- commissioned report or evaluation;
- advisory committee or reference group;
- policy-focused research grant;
- public dataset or diagnostic;
- implementation study;
- commentary, correspondence, op-ed, or practitioner article;
- stakeholder convening;
- industry-policy research partnership.

## Policy free-work boundary

A public-interest submission may legitimately be unpaid when it advances the user's policy platform, public mission, or evidence base. Still require proportionality and attribution.

Do not provide repeated bespoke advice, private drafting, confidential analysis, lobbying strategy, or implementation support without a clear role, mandate, and resource arrangement.

## Impact evidence ladder

Record evidence conservatively:

1. submission or contribution delivered;
2. acknowledgement or meeting secured;
3. repeat access or invitation;
4. citation, quotation, or use in official material;
5. change in draft wording, guidance, process, or decision discussion;
6. implementation, evaluation, or funded follow-on activity;
7. durable advisory relationship or policy network position.

Do not claim that a policy outcome was caused by the user's work unless evidence supports attribution.

## Policy hard gates

Stop or redesign when:

- there is no identifiable decision or policy process;
- the engagement is mainly symbolic visibility;
- the user is expected to conceal private interests;
- the requested role conflicts with editorial, research, advisory, or public duties;
- the user cannot retain intellectual independence;
- the workload is substantial but no public, scholarly, relational, or policy asset will remain;
- the process is closed and no plausible access or influence pathway exists;
- a firm expects the user to lobby for regulatory relief without an explicit, legitimate, and disclosed role;
- the user would need to overstate evidence or suppress material uncertainty.

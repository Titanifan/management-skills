# Provenance Map for Strategic Opportunity

## Purpose and claim boundary

Use this file to distinguish the origins of Strategic Opportunity from the design of its executable workflow. Sources provide constructs, mechanisms, or practitioner lenses; they do not validate the complete Skill, its scores, its verdicts, or its application to an individual academic career.

Most scholarly sources theorise firms, organisational units, or corporate managers. Applying them to a scholar's opportunity portfolio is a cross-level translation. Treat scores as disciplined elicitation devices, not estimated causal models.

## Provenance labels

- `Scholarly-direct`: the source directly develops the named construct, mechanism, or distinction.
- `Scholarly-translated`: the executable rule adapts scholarship across level or context.
- `Practitioner-derived`: the rule comes from a practitioner framework whose assumptions must be tested.
- `Authorial-synthesis`: the repository combines sources into a workflow, score, threshold, classification, or artefact.
- `Case-calibrated`: repeated documented cases support a behaviour under stated conditions. No v0.2 element has this status.

## Source-to-rule map

| Element | Provenance | What the source supports | Executable use in this Skill | Boundary condition or failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy as an evolving pattern | `Scholarly-direct` | Strategies can be understood as patterns in streams of decisions, with deliberate and emergent forms (Mintzberg, 1978; Mintzberg & Waters, 1985). | Examine the path of commitments and assets, not only the stated plan. | Retrospective pattern recognition does not determine the best opportunity ex ante. |
| Exploration and exploitation | `Scholarly-direct` | Organisational learning involves competing exploration and exploitation demands (March, 1991). | Preserve exploration without granting every uncertain idea core status. | The source does not prescribe a universal portfolio balance or an individual allocation rule. |
| Resource heterogeneity and defensibility | `Scholarly-direct` | Valuable, rare, difficult-to-imitate, and non-substitutable resources can underpin sustained advantage (Barney, 1991). | Ask which reusable assets an opportunity could create. | A personal reputation, relationship, or dataset is not automatically a defensible firm resource. |
| Attention structures | `Scholarly-direct` | Organisational rules and relationships shape which issues and answers receive attention (Ocasio, 1997). | Treat attention crowding and repeated issue exposure as selection costs. | Attention is organisationally structured and cannot always be reallocated by individual choice. |
| Behavioural bounds and cognitively distant opportunities | `Scholarly-direct` | Behavioural strategy examines how cognition shapes search for strategically valuable opportunities (Gavetti, 2012). | Test whether an overlooked opportunity is both non-consensus and accessible to this actor. | Novelty or cognitive distance is not evidence of value. |
| Dynamic managerial capabilities | `Scholarly-direct` | Managerial cognition, human capital, and social capital can contribute to heterogeneous strategic outcomes (Adner & Helfat, 2003). | Examine actor-specific capability and access rather than abstract opportunity quality alone. | Current capability can be built, borrowed, or delegated; corporate-level evidence does not validate a personal-fit score. |
| Internal venturing and strategic context | `Scholarly-direct` | Internal initiatives need selection and strategic context to influence corporate strategy (Burgelman, 1983). | Distinguish a locally interesting initiative from one that can secure ownership, legitimacy, and resources. | Internal corporate venturing does not transfer automatically to independent ventures, policy, or academic work. |
| Sequential investment under uncertainty | `Scholarly-direct` | Real-options reasoning can inform incremental positioning investments under uncertainty (McGrath, 1997). | Prefer opportunities whose central uncertainty can be tested before irreversible build. | Calling an activity an option does not make it cheap, reversible, informative, or worth preserving. |
| Opportunity-seeking plus advantage-seeking | `Scholarly-direct` | Strategic entrepreneurship integrates entrepreneurial opportunity-seeking with strategic advantage-seeking (Hitt et al., 2001; Ireland et al., 2003). | Require both a credible opportunity and a path to durable value creation or capture. | These models do not validate the Skill's scores or Peter Thiel's seven questions. |
| Opportunity discovery versus creation | `Scholarly-direct` | Discovery and creation theories imply different assumptions and entrepreneurial actions (Alvarez & Barney, 2007). | Ask whether evidence can precede action or whether bounded action must help create the opportunity. | A case can contain both logics; the distinction does not itself determine whether the opportunity deserves entry. |
| Personal arena and compounding-asset rule | `Scholarly-translated` | Strategy-pattern, resource, attention, and capability mechanisms jointly motivate cumulative path analysis. | Prefer opportunities that deepen an arena and create reusable assets rather than isolated activity. | This synthesis crosses from firm and organisation levels to a personal portfolio and requires case calibration. |
| Actor-specific opportunity fit | `Scholarly-translated` | Dynamic managerial capabilities and attention research make actor heterogeneity and constraints relevant. | Assess the user's role, access, ownership, capability, and cognitive cost. | A weak current fit can sometimes be redesigned; a strong fit does not make a weak opportunity attractive. |
| Option-preserving selection rule | `Scholarly-translated` | Real-options logic supports sequential commitments when learning is possible. | Pass positive or provisional opportunities to Adaptive Commitment rather than designing execution here. | Additional stages can create delay, ritual, and governance cost without useful learning. |
| Seven venture questions | `Practitioner-derived` | Thiel and Masters (2014) offer an adversarial venture lens covering engineering, timing, monopoly, people, distribution, durability, and secret. | Stress-test venture architecture after the opportunity passes the rapid screen. | This is not peer-reviewed validation. The lens favours concentration, private value capture, founder control, and monopoly-oriented reasoning. |
| Strategic score, thresholds, and dispositions | `Authorial-synthesis` | No listed source specifies this scoring system. | Structure comparison and force an explicit portfolio disposition. | Numerical precision can conceal weak evidence; hard gates and narrative diagnosis override totals. |
| PIP/ZVP dual-track architecture | `Authorial-synthesis` | The repository combines practice-impact tests with the practitioner venture lens. | Keep public or organisational impact separate from scalable, defensible, ownable venture potential. | Neither track has been validated as a unified predictive model. |
| Score weights, caps, overrides, and two-by-two classes | `Authorial-synthesis` | These are repository design choices. | Prevent absent buyers, weak evidence, false market definitions, or unavailable ownership from being averaged away. | Thresholds require forward tests and case calibration; they must not be presented as empirical cut-offs. |

## Calibration rules

- Do not convert a citation into authority for a specific verdict.
- Do not infer causality from conceptual or process research.
- Do not treat a firm-level construct as automatically valid at the individual level.
- Do not let adjacent scholarship academically legitimise a practitioner thesis.
- Record decision quality separately from realised outcome.
- Reserve `Case-calibrated` for a documented series of comparable cases, not one vivid success.
